# YouTube niche scrub for a faceless Sowards Consulting channel

Written November 14, 2026. Sources: the Pocket recording "Faceless YouTube Channel Prompt Walkthrough" (November 13), the August 2026 de-escalation launch plan and competitive scout in Dropbox, the course catalog and weekly briefs in this repository, one recorded instructor lecture, and a five-lane web scrub run November 13 and 14. Channel numbers come from third-party trackers (vidIQ, Social Blade, SponsorRadar, CreatorDB) on the dates shown, not from YouTube itself, so treat them as approximate. Anything that could not be confirmed is marked unverified.

The goal this document scores against: a channel that can realistically reach monetization, build a following, and feed Sowards Consulting (Briefing Room subscriptions, STC course seats, workshop and expert bookings). De-escalation is one candidate among several, not the starting assumption.

## 1. The short answer

Build one faceless channel whose engine is the weekly use of force case law brief that already exists, and give it three content pillars:

1. **The weekly case law update.** Appellate use of force decisions explained for the people who have to live with them. Nobody on YouTube does this. The brief, the narration script, and the cloned-voice audio are already produced every week, so the added cost is visuals and editing.
2. **"Why the county paid."** Jail, prison, and juvenile facility lawsuits broken down at practitioner level: what the policy said, what the training was, what the jury saw. This is the growth hook. The "cops get sued" lane is enormous but entirely patrol-focused and civilian-framed. Custody litigation has no video explainer at all.
3. **Staff-side corrections survival.** Downing the Duck and staff manipulation, PREA obligations, report writing, Title 15, first-year survival. The inmate-perspective genre has a dozen channels near or above a million subscribers. The staff side has none with a measurable count.

De-escalation stays as an occasional topic inside pillar 1 and 3, not as the channel identity. The reasons are in section 4.

Narration should be Dr. Sowards' own voice, recorded or cloned. YouTube's disclosure page lists "cloning one's own voice to create voice overs or dubs" as content that does not need an AI label. An AI avatar or a synthetic "host" presented as a legal expert is named in YouTube's July 2026 monetization policy as ineligible for ads, so the channel should never use one.

## 2. What was already decided, and what changed

The August 2026 plan in Dropbox was an on-camera, cross-industry de-escalation channel: Dr. Sowards talking to the lens, verticals for healthcare, schools, and workplaces, a quick-reference card as lead magnet, and B2B workshops as the money. It was a reasonable plan for an on-camera channel.

Three things changed:

- The ask is now faceless. The August plan's entire edge was "your face and credential on screen. Trust is the product." Without the face, de-escalation becomes a content lane where the scale belongs to on-camera personalities (Jefferson Fisher at roughly 1.1 million subscribers, Chase Hughes at roughly 1.8 million, both as of September to November 2026 tracker data) and the rest is institutional explainers from CPI, hospital systems, and liability trusts.
- Since August the repository has shipped 14 weekly briefs covering more than 60 decided cases, a narration pipeline in Dr. Sowards' cloned voice, a podcast on Apple and Spotify, and a paid Briefing Room membership. That is a finished weekly production line that the August plan did not have.
- Grok is already drafting and scheduling posts across Instagram, Facebook, LinkedIn, YouTube, and TikTok from the weekly case law topic. A YouTube video from the same brief rides distribution that already runs.

The four view-count claims in the August plan (Dartmouth video at 531K, Texas medical video at 36K, a TEDx talk at 36K, a public safety video at 178K) could not be verified in this scrub. The videos were identified but their counts were not retrievable. The claim that "no credentialed human owns de-escalation on YouTube" is half right: no de-escalation specialist has a large channel, but adjacent communication personalities do.

### Lane A: use of force case law decisions explained

What exists. The faceless giants in the police lane are all incident-first body cam channels: PoliceActivity (about 6.9 million subscribers, vidIQ, November 13, 2026), Code Blue Cam (about 3.5 million), EWU Bodycam (about 3.7 million), Audit the Audit (about 3.0 million), LackLuster (about 1.3 to 1.5 million, 2024 to 2025 snapshots), Real World Police (about 1.6 million, April 2025). Audit the Audit is the closest existing model: a faceless narrator over footage, citing statutes and case law on screen and grading the officer. It is still incident-first, and its growth has plateaued at roughly one video every five days.

The decision-level commentators are on camera: The Civil Rights Lawyer (about 2.2 million, the fastest grower in the set at plus 70,000 subscribers in October 2026) is plaintiff-side and litigates his own cases. Steve Lehto (about 620,000) is consumer law with occasional police cases. LegalEagle (about 3.9 million) is general legal entertainment.

The training-industry channels are small and on camera: Blue to Gold (about 30,000 subscribers, 1,665 videos, search and seizure focus), Street Cop Training (about 63,000), Police1 (about 21,000), Lexipol (about 3,200), Calibre Press (about 9,500 and dormant). Every one of them uses YouTube as a funnel for paid courses, which is the right model for this channel too.

The gap. Searches for a channel that explains appellate use of force decisions for officers, trainers, corrections staff, or students returned nothing. Searches for video coverage of Kingsley v. Hendrickson, Whitley v. Albers, or Hudson v. McMillian returned only text case briefs. California PC 835a and AB 392 have one authoritative video, POST's own, and it sits behind the POST learning portal login. Corrections1 publishes "the use of force case every correctional officer should know" as text only. Case names, circuit numbers, and holdings do not appear in the titles of any channel in the cohort.

Why it fits. The brief already orders every week the same way (Ninth Circuit, Supreme Court, other circuits, California, application) and ends every case with "in practice" and "train it." The narration script exists. The audio exists in Dr. Sowards' voice. The weekly cadence is already being met. The cost of adding YouTube is visuals and an edit.

The risk. Case law is dry if titled like a law review. The lane's proven title grammar is outcome verbs in capitals ("LOSES Qualified Immunity," "WINS in Court"), the qualified immunity question hook ("Officer Gets Qualified Immunity?"), and explicit follow-up labels ("UPDATE," "Part 2"). The brief's content supports those hooks; the titles have to use them. See section 7.

### Lane B: custody lawsuit and settlement breakdowns

What exists. Local news stations run jail death settlement packages every month: Hennepin County $3.4 million, San Diego County $16 million with 55 hours of jail video not preserved, El Paso County (Colorado) $2.5 million, Riverside County $1.8 million, Allen County (Indiana) $1.1 million, all 2024 to 2026. Law&Crime (about 7.6 million subscribers, June 2026) and Court TV cover trials. The accountability channels cover patrol stops. Nobody synthesizes custody litigation for the people who work in custody.

The gap. No channel was found doing faceless, practitioner-level breakdowns of jail, prison, juvenile hall, or PREA litigation aimed at staff and trainers. The "what should the deputy or JCO have done, per policy and case law" angle is open. The briefs already contain the raw material: a $33 million jail death verdict left in place by the Supreme Court in October 2026 (Bond v. Sheriff of Ottawa County), a $21 million Taser judgment (Edwards v. Grubbs), a jail overdose death and Monell liability (Johnson v. Tarrant County), restraint to a fixed object under the Eighth Amendment (Mason v. Talley), children locked in holding cells (Rockett v. Eighmy), failure to protect in cell assignment (Bonner v. Ward), jail medical failure to train (Byrum v. Knox County).

Why it fits. Dollar-first titles are the strongest proven hook in the adjacent lane, and the practitioner framing is the differentiation. This pillar is also the one most likely to be forwarded by training coordinators, risk managers, and county counsel, which is the audience that buys agency course bundles.

The risk. Three rules keep this pillar safe. Closed cases only: verdicts, settlements, and appellate opinions, never pending matters. Never a case in which Dr. Sowards is or has been retained, in either direction. No active-incident reaction, which the August plan already flagged because opposing counsel will use public opinions in depositions. Defamation exposure is managed by quoting court records and settlement language, attributing allegations, and noting no-admission clauses. Advertiser suitability is managed by keeping footage non-graphic and the framing educational; YouTube's own guidelines put "violent, combative, or abrasive interactions with law enforcement in an educational context" in the full-ads tier and "ultra graphic violent acts" in the no-ads tier.

PREA staff-obligation content: zero channels found, only government PDFs and DOJ videos. Juvenile facility staff content: zero channels found.

Why it fits. The six-stage Downing the Duck framework is written, the Manipulation and Grooming course and the PREA course exist, and the instructor lecture shows how these are actually taught. The audience is roughly 390,000 correctional officers and 92,000 probation officers in the United States (BLS, 2024), plus 1,277 juvenile residential facilities (OJJDP, 2022). Every one of them has a training coordinator with a budget.

The risk. PREA and staff sexual misconduct topics sit in YouTube's "controversial issues" category. Since January 2026, non-graphic discussion of sexual abuse earns full ads, but content involving minors raises the bar, and the thumbnail and first 15 seconds are what the classifier weighs. Keep it educational, keep it about the staff member's obligations and warning signs, and never use identifiable youth.

### Lane D: de-escalation

Demand is real and cross-industry (the "crisis intervention training" term alone shows about 5,400 US searches a month; "crisis intervention team" about 49,500). The supply has two layers: institutional explainers that are dry (CPI, TMLT, Dartmouth, police departments) and on-camera communication personalities that are enormous (Fisher, Hughes, The Behavior Panel at about 1.2 million). A faceless channel cannot beat the personalities on general-audience "what to say when" content, and the institutional layer is where a faceless educational channel would land. The one sub-vertical with no supply found is custody de-escalation, which is already covered by pillar 1 (de-escalation is the feasibility element of PC 835a and appears in half the cases) and pillar 3.

Keep the August scripts. "The first three seconds," "stop saying calm down," and "body language that de-escalates" work as Shorts and as the de-escalation segment of a case video. Do not make them the channel.

## 4. What "faceless" should mean here

Faceless means no on-camera host. It does not mean anonymous, and it should not mean voiceless.

- **Voice.** Dr. Sowards' own voice, either recorded or the existing ElevenLabs clone. YouTube's disclosure page says cloning one's own voice for voice-overs does not require a label, and a disclosure label does not affect monetization or recommendation anyway. The voice is the credential; a stock AI narrator throws it away and moves the channel toward the profile YouTube removed in its January 2026 enforcement wave (synthetic narration, templated thumbnails, stock-footage loops).
- **No AI avatar.** YouTube's July 2026 policy names "AI personas giving legal advice or interpreting laws" as content that will not be allowed to monetize. The HeyGen avatar used in the courses should not host the channel.
- **Credential on screen.** The channel About page, a short animated open, and a corner badge carry the name and credential. A small real headshot in the thumbnail is allowed and builds trust without making it an on-camera channel. This is a decision for Dr. Sowards (see section 9).
- **Visuals.** Court opinion text with highlighted holdings, docket stamps, a timeline graphic for the facts, a circuit map, brand slides in the navy, red, and gold from the brand kit, the training photos already in the app, and body camera footage only when it is public record or a court exhibit, non-graphic, and obtained through formal records channels. A sworn trainer must not use agency footage without a records request; an Ohio officer was fired in 2025 for posting body cam he downloaded at work.
- **Originality.** Every video is bespoke analysis of a specific decision or case. That is the "original, authentic insights or perspective" YouTube's inauthentic-content policy requires, and it is the one thing content farms cannot copy.

## 5. Monetization: where the money actually is

Ad revenue is the smallest piece. Measured 2026 data from 300 channels puts the Education and Science median RPM at $10.22, with a wide spread ($2.31 to $19.50 between the 25th and 75th percentiles), and vendor estimates for legal education run $8 to $22. At those rates a channel needs millions of views a year before ads matter. Plan for ads as a bonus.

The ladder, in order of value:

1. **Briefing Room membership** at $9.99 a month or $59.99 a year. Every video ends with the same line: the full brief, the roll call print, and the knowledge check are in the app.
2. **STC course seats.** Agency bundles from $750 for ten seats to $6,500 for a hundred. The "why the county paid" videos are the natural pitch for Defensible Use of Force, The Last Line (supervisor review), and the two Ironclad courses. The staff-side videos pitch Manipulation and Grooming, PREA, and Title 15.
3. **Workshops and speaking.** The August plan's B2B ladder still applies: the channel is the credibility reel that gets a department or conference to book the in-person course.
4. **Expert and consulting leads.** Attorneys and risk managers search case names. A channel that explains custody decisions accurately is how they find the practitioner who can testify about training and policy. This is high value per lead and needs the closed-cases-only rule to stay clean.
5. **YouTube ads and fan funding** last.

Timing on the YouTube Partner Program. Today's ad threshold is 1,000 subscribers plus 4,000 watch hours in 12 months (or 10 million Shorts views in 90 days). From February 1, 2027, new applicants reportedly need 8,000 watch hours, double the current bar; current members are grandfathered. Reaching 1,000 subscribers and 4,000 hours from zero before January 31, 2027 is unlikely in eleven weeks, so plan for the 2027 bar and treat the fan-funding tier (500 subscribers, 3,000 watch hours) as the first milestone. This changes nothing about the real money, which is items 1 through 4.

### Prompt 1: competitor analysis (three competitors)

The three channels closest to the recommended lane, and what to take from each.

**Audit the Audit** (faceless, about 3.0 million subscribers, 544 videos since May 2019, roughly one video every five days, growth flat in October 2026).
- Pillars: traffic stops and First Amendment audits, graded encounter by encounter, with statute and case citations on screen.

**The Civil Rights Lawyer** (on camera, about 2.2 million, 944 videos since January 2020, about two a week, plus 70,000 subscribers in October 2026).
- Pillars: his own Section 1983 cases, qualified immunity rulings, body cam breakdowns.

**Blue to Gold** (on camera, about 30,000 subscribers, 1,665 videos since 2018, numbered Q&A episodes, course funnel for a company that says it has trained 90,000 officers).

- Title patterns: the officer's actual question as the title.
- Gaps: search and seizure, not force; police, not corrections or probation; tiny YouTube footprint for a large training business.
- Take: this is the business model (YouTube as the top of a paid training funnel) and the format for Shorts (the question an officer actually asks, answered in 60 seconds). Blue to Gold proves the model works commercially even with a small channel, which is the honest expectation for year one.

### Prompt 5: publishing system under 1,000 subscribers

Stage: zero subscribers, no videos, no best-performing video yet. Goal: establish, then monetize through the ladder in section 5, not through ads.

Cadence. One long video a week (10 to 15 minutes) cut from the weekly brief, published Sunday evening with the email so it lands before Monday roll call. Three Shorts a week cut from it: one case in 60 seconds, one "train it" rule, one career or staff-survival clip. One pillar 2 or pillar 3 long video a month on top of the weekly, so the channel has 5 long videos and 12 to 15 Shorts a month.

Production. The brief JSON produces the narration script and the audio today. Add a step that produces a shot list from the same JSON (facts, holding, in practice, train it, quiz) so the editor assembles slides, opinion highlights, and timelines against the audio. Budget the edit to a freelancer at $20 to $40 a video, as the August plan proposed, so Dr. Sowards' hours go to review, not editing.

Repurposing. A generated "social kit" file next to each brief: verified case facts, holding, train-it points, the video link, and a five-day post schedule. Grok formats and schedules from that file, Dr. Sowards approves, and nothing invented about a case reaches a platform. Shorts go to Instagram Reels, TikTok, and Facebook on the same schedule.

Measurement. Watch time and average view duration on the long videos; click-through rate on thumbnails; Briefing Room sign-ups and course previews started from the video description link, which are the numbers that matter. Subscribers are the lagging indicator.

- Weeks 1 to 2: channel name and About page, brand open and end screen, badge decision, one pilot video from the current brief, three Shorts.
- Weeks 3 to 8: weekly video plus Shorts, every week; publish the first "why the county paid" video (Bond, $33 million) and the first Downing the Duck video; add the social kit step to the repository build.

## 8. Risks and the rules that manage them

- **Expert witness credibility.** Closed cases only. Never a case in which Dr. Sowards is or was retained. No reaction to active incidents. Educational framing, standards and hypotheticals, as the August plan already required.
- **Accuracy.** Every case verified against the opinion before recording, which the briefs already do and flag in the caution field. Garbled case names in lecture transcripts must not reach a script.
- **Footage.** Public records or court exhibits only, obtained through formal channels, non-graphic, no identifiable juveniles, no agency footage without a records request. Expect occasional false copyright strikes from TV stations on public footage; keep the records request on file.
- **YouTube policy.** Human or own-cloned voice, no AI avatar, bespoke analysis per video, non-graphic visuals, educational framing. These keep the channel in the full-ads tier and outside the inauthentic-content rules.
- **Ad suitability on sensitive topics.** PREA and abuse topics are monetizable when non-graphic and discussed, but the thumbnail and first 15 seconds carry the weight. Lead with the staff obligation, not the abuse.
- **Expectation.** The training-industry channels that run this business model (Blue to Gold, Street Cop Training) have 30,000 to 63,000 subscribers after years of uploads and run profitable companies on them. A realistic year-one target is the fan-funding tier and a measurable lift in Briefing Room and course sign-ups, not a million subscribers.

## 9. Decisions for Dr. Sowards
