# Case library: where the course materials and the opinions differ

Internal notes for Dr. Sowards, written November 15, 2026 while the first 27 Case library briefs were checked against the opinions. Each brief states the holding as the court wrote it and keeps the teaching lesson separate. These are the places where the course outlines, the 40-hour lecture deck, or the Force of Law books describe a case more broadly than the court did. None of this changes the lessons; it changes how they should be attributed.

## Attribution and captions

- "Reasonably well trained officer" comes from Malley v. Briggs, 475 U.S. 335, 345 (1986), quoting United States v. Leon. Anderson v. Creighton, 483 U.S. 635 (1987), uses "reasonable officer" and adds the requirement that the right be clearly established in a particularized sense. The Anderson dissent quotes a law review phrase, "the average well-trained police officer," which may be the source of the mix-up.
- The D.C. Circuit case is styled Parker v. District of Columbia, 850 F.2d 708 (D.C. Cir. 1988), not District of Columbia v. Parker.
- The 1999 Ninth Circuit case is Saman v. Robbins (plaintiff Yousef Saman; the decedent is spelled Butres Samaan in the opinion). "Samman" is not the court's spelling.
- Rymer v. Davis: the full history is 754 F.2d 198 (6th Cir. 1985), vacated and remanded, City of Shepherdsville v. Rymer, 105 S. Ct. 3518 (1985), reaffirmed on remand, 775 F.2d 756 (6th Cir. 1985).
- Reed v. Hoy: the amended opinion is reported at 909 F.2d 324; the archive copy read carries 891 F.2d 1421 with the July 18, 1990 amendment.

## Holdings described more broadly than the court wrote them

- Whiteley v. Warden, 401 U.S. 560 (1971), is a Fourth Amendment arrest warrant and probable cause case on habeas corpus. It says nothing about training records or reports. The documentation lesson ("if it is not documented, it did not occur") is an analogy to two things the Court did: it refused to let an insufficient affidavit be rehabilitated with facts the affiant knew but did not write down, and it refused the State a remand to build a record it had not built. The brief says so.
- Hays v. Jefferson County, 668 F.2d 869 (6th Cir. 1982): the opinion does not use the word "unstructured" and says nothing about documenting the nature, scope, and quality of training. It is a standard-setting case (simple negligence is not enough; a complete failure to train or grossly negligent training with at least implicit authorization or acquiescence) that vacated the verdicts for a new trial.
- City of Canton v. Harris, 489 U.S. 378 (1989), adopted the deliberate indifference standard and remanded. It did not hold the city liable and did not speak to documenting training. Its footnote on deadly force training supports the "train to the tasks officers actually face" lesson.
- Bordanaro v. McLeod, 871 F.2d 1151 (1st Cir. 1989): the court's phrase is "recruitment, training, supervision or discipline." The word retention does not appear; the failure to discipline and the delayed investigation are the closest the court comes.
- Parker v. District of Columbia: the court announced no duty to keep officers physically fit or to train in disarming and subduing. It held that a jury could find deliberate indifference on this record, which included four years without physical training and a demonstration that the officer could have subdued Parker without a firearm. It was a 2 to 1 decision before Canton.
- Owens v. Haas, 601 F.2d 1242 (2d Cir. 1979), is a pleading-stage reversal that allowed discovery. It did not find the county liable or the training inadequate, and its gross negligence language predates Canton.
- Zuchel v. City and County of Denver, 997 F.2d 730 (10th Cir. 1993), did not declare lecture-only training inadequate as a rule; it held the evidence sufficient for the jury under Canton.
- Wright v. City of Euclid, 962 F.3d 852 (6th Cir. 2020), reversed summary judgment; nothing was decided against the city. The court said what the officers knew before the force controls, so the medical condition mattered only if they knew of it.
- Forrester v. City of San Diego, 25 F.3d 804 (9th Cir. 1994): the court held that substantial evidence supported the defense verdict and declined to rule on the policy's constitutionality. The 40-hour deck's line that pain compliance on passive resisters "was objectively reasonable" overstates it. The court spelled the device "Orcutt Police Nonchakus."
- Scott v. Henrich, 39 F.3d 912 (9th Cir. 1994): the least intrusive alternative discussion was aimed at the approach to the door; the widow had abandoned her challenge to the shooting itself. The court also warned judges not to simply accept a surviving officer's own account.
- Reed v. Hoy: the October 2026 correction ("not a retreat case") overcorrects slightly. The principal holding is the Graham objective test and the plain error in the malice instruction, but Part III.B separately holds that under Oregon law the duty to retreat does not extend to officers performing their official function. It establishes no federal rule on retreat; California's rule is Penal Code section 835a.
- Deorle v. Rutherford, 272 F.3d 1272 (9th Cir. 2001): Force of Law Volume I says Deorle was "carrying a container and a hatchet he had put down" and "had committed no crime." The opinion says he threw the hatchet into the trees on command, discarded the crossbow when told to, carried only a can or bottle when shot, and was charged under Penal Code section 69; the court called the crime, if any, minor. Deorle's deadly force discussion uses the Vera Cruz definition later overruled by Smith v. City of Hemet.
- Smith v. City of Hemet, 394 F.3d 689 (9th Cir. 2005) (en banc): the course outlines are accurate. Both Force of Law volumes are not. They say the court held 8 to 3 that a police dog bite capable of serious injury is deadly force, that dog deployments are analyzed like firearms, that the majority found the deployment reasonable, and that the decision date was January 14, 2005. The court expressly declined to decide whether the dog was deadly force, held only that a reasonable jury could find the force excessive, left qualified immunity open, and filed the opinion January 10, 2005. The books also describe Vera Cruz as holding that dog bites are not deadly force; Smith describes Vera Cruz as defining deadly force as force reasonably likely to kill.
- Saman v. Robbins: the kick analysis rests on the officer's own account because the plaintiff's beating account did not implicate that officer. The case does not approve strikes on a handcuffed, controlled person.
- Kingsley v. Hendrickson, 576 U.S. 389 (2015), set the objective standard and vacated for a bad instruction. It did not hold the Taser use excessive and left the Eighth Amendment standard for convicted prisoners untouched.
- Hudson v. McMillian, 503 U.S. 1 (1992): no serious injury threshold, but the force must still be malicious and sadistic, and de minimis force is excluded. Only five Justices joined the extension of Whitley to all force claims.
- Whitley v. Albers, 475 U.S. 312 (1986): the malicious and sadistic test was announced for disturbances; Hudson extended it. Whitley reinstated a directed verdict; it did not hold the plan correct.
- Saucier v. Katz, 533 U.S. 194 (2001): the mandatory two step sequence became discretionary in Pearson v. Callahan, 555 U.S. 223 (2009), confirmed from Pearson's text.
- City and County of San Francisco v. Sheehan, 575 U.S. 600 (2015): the Court did not decide whether the ADA applies to arrests (that question was dismissed) and did not decide whether the failure to accommodate violated the Fourth Amendment. It decided only that no clearly established law required accommodation before reentry, and said departing from training does not by itself defeat qualified immunity.
- County of Los Angeles v. Mendez, 581 U.S. 420 (2017): rejected the provocation rule and made Graham the exclusive test for the force itself, but declined to decide whether pre-shooting conduct can be weighed within Graham's totality, and sent proximate cause on the warrantless entry back to the Ninth Circuit.
- Lombardo v. City of St. Louis, 594 U.S. 464 (2021): a per curiam vacatur. It did not hold the prone restraint unconstitutional, set no duration or weight limit, and did not decide qualified immunity. It held only that resistance cannot be treated as controlling per se.
- Tennessee v. Garner, 471 U.S. 1 (1985): the conclusion says "significant threat of death or serious physical injury"; the operative rule says "threat of serious physical harm" and requires necessity and a warning where feasible. Officer Hymon was already out of the case on qualified immunity; Garner is not a verdict against anyone.
- Graham v. Connor, 490 U.S. 386 (1989): the three factors are introduced by "including," as examples within a totality inquiry. The Court did not decide whether the force on Graham was excessive and left open the Fourth Amendment's reach into pretrial detention.

## Second batch, November 15 (36 briefs)

- Bryan v. MacPherson: the court spells the officer MacPherson; the course materials say McPherson. The holding is limited to dart mode Tasers and devices with similar effects; the en banc court in Mattos declined to classify drive stun mode.
- Mattos v. Agarano and Brooks v. City of Seattle: the court held a reasonable fact finder could find both Taser uses excessive on the plaintiffs' versions, then granted qualified immunity on the federal claims. "Both uses held excessive" overstates the posture. Brooks's Washington assault and battery claim survived.
- Headwaters Forest Defense: the course cites the May 4, 2000 decision. That opinion was amended in 2001, vacated by the Supreme Court in light of Saucier, and the 2002 opinion after remand is the one holding that no reasonable officer could have thought the pepper spray reasonable. The 2000 opinion only sent the question to a jury. The protesters were linked with steel "black bear" devices, not locked to logs.
- Blankenhorn v. City of Orange: the failure to train evidence against the city failed (it concerned one officer); what survived was the supervisory claim against the chief for approving evaluations despite earlier force complaints.
- Jackson v. City of Bremerton: the holding is that no Fourth Amendment violation occurred; qualified immunity is a footnote alternative. The spray hit her hair, and the court called the intrusion minimal.
- Plakas v. Drinski: the no alternatives holding stands, but its statement that the court judges the shooting "from that point on" is timing language that Barnes v. Felix (2025) rejects as a rule. Do not teach Plakas as the current rule on timing.
- Scott v. Smith: the Supreme Court's April 20, 2026 order vacates and remands in light of Zorn with no reasoning; it does not mention Drummond and does not hold the force reasonable or the officers immune. The Ninth Circuit opinion is not good law to cite.
- Zorn v. Linton: decided only the clearly established prong; it did not approve the wristlock. The majority and dissent disagree on whether the warning came before the hold.
- Rochin v. California was a criminal evidence case. Lewis calls it the origin of the shocks the conscience test and says it would be a Fourth Amendment case today.
- County of Sacramento v. Lewis says nothing approving the deputy's driving ("prudence would have repressed the reaction"); its directive to decide the constitutional question first was later made discretionary by Pearson.
- Estate of Hernandez: all eleven judges found the first two volleys reasonable as a matter of law; the split was over the third. Teach the baseline holding for the officer along with the reassessment point.
- Hart v. City of Grand Rapids: qualified immunity for the launcher pointing and pepper spray rested on the clearly established prong only; the deadly force characterization of the launcher shot rested on the department's own training testimony. Sixth Circuit factors are circuit specific.
- Hainze v. Richards: Title II does not apply before the scene is secured, but the court said the duty to accommodate attaches to handling and transport once it is. The force claim was decided under Heck, not on reasonableness. Fifth Circuit law.
- Pena v. Leombruni does not discuss a duty to retreat; it says special measures might be desirable and that failing to adopt them is at most negligence.
- Pierce v. Multnomah County: the Fourth Amendment governs a warrantless arrestee until release or a judicial finding of probable cause, not all pretrial detention; Kingsley covers the later stage.
- Cunningham v. Gates does not apply the provocation rule; footnote 23 declines to endorse tactics that needlessly create danger. No finding was made on the shootings.
- Rodriguez v. County of Los Angeles: the pretrial detainee was tried under the Eighth Amendment instruction; the court said Kingsley should have applied but the error was forfeited and harmless. Monell liability rested on a custom of failing to investigate and discipline.
- Chinaryan v. City of Los Angeles: the court held a jury could find a violation and that the law was clearly established; it did not hold the officers violated the Fourth Amendment. The city won the Monell trial. Filed August 14, 2024.
- Monell is a maternity leave case, not a force case, and did not create failure to train liability.
- Tatum v. City and County of San Francisco: the docket is 04-15055, not 03-16286. Tatum never cites Drummond; any comparison is a teaching comparison. The CPR holding is narrow: promptly summoning medical help satisfied the Fourth Amendment on that record.
- Alves v. County of Riverside: the jury found no Fourth Amendment excessive force; the holding is only that the negligence verdict could be reconciled with that finding under Hayes. The court did not hold prone restraint unreasonable or require a recovery position.
- Spencer v. Pew: qualified immunity was granted for everything up to handcuffing on the clearly established prong; immunity was denied only for the post handcuffing knee and body weight. The other officers were not liable under the integral participant doctrine.
- Robinson v. Solano County: the deputies won qualified immunity (the law was not clearly established in 1995). It establishes the principle on pointing a gun; it is not a liability case.
- Kostrzewa v. City of Troy is a motion to dismiss decision: allegations only, Sixth Circuit, persuasive in California.
- LaLonde v. County of Riverside: its statement that the immunity test equals the merits test was overruled by Saucier; the force holdings remain good law.
- Washington v. Lambert: one of the few cases where liability was established (directed verdict affirmed). The court did not decide whether reasonable suspicion existed; Judge Kozinski did not join the discussion of race.
- Meredith v. Erath: the agent was immune for the decision to handcuff during an evidence search, not for the force or for 30 minutes of tight cuffs after complaints.
- Palmer, Wall, Meredith, LaLonde, Drummond, Spencer, and Kostrzewa all arrive on summary judgment, judgment as a matter of law, or a motion to dismiss; none is a finding of liability.
- Three opinions could not be read because the Ninth Circuit's archive is missing them: Gregory v. County of Maui, Andrews v. City of Henderson, and O'Doan v. Sanford. They wait on a source with the California cases.
