Smith versus City of Hemet. From the Sowards Briefing Room. Hemet officers responding to a domestic violence call pepper sprayed a man in pajamas on his own porch who repeatedly refused commands, took him to the ground, dragged him off the porch, and had a police dog bite him three times, then he pleaded guilty to resisting under Penal Code section 148(a)(1) and sued. The en banc Ninth Circuit reversed summary judgment. It held the suit was not barred by Heck v. Humphrey because the record did not show whether his plea rested on his pre-arrest obstruction or his resistance during the arrest; held a jury could find the force excessive; and redefined deadly force in the circuit as force that creates a substantial risk of causing death or serious bodily injury, overruling Vera Cruz v. City of Escondido. It did not decide whether the dog was deadly force. It is taught for the deadly force definition, which matches California Penal Code section 835a.

Smith versus City of Hemet. Ninth Circuit, en banc, decided January 10, 2005, docket number 02-56445. This is a published decision.

Here are the facts. The court said the facts of the encounter were not seriously disputed and took the plaintiff's version where they differed. On the night of August 16, 1999, Thomas Smith's wife called the Hemet Police Department and reported that her husband was hitting her or was physical with her. She told emergency personnel that he had no gun, that there were no weapons in the house, and that he was in his pajamas. Officer Reinbolt arrived first and saw Smith on his front porch with his hands in his pockets. He announced himself and told Smith to remove his hands from his pockets. Smith refused, swore at him, and told him to come to him. Reinbolt said he would approach only after Smith showed his hands. Smith refused again and went inside. He came back out with his hands still in his pockets. Told again to show his hands, he did, but then refused an order to put his hands on his head and walk toward the officer's voice, and again asked the officer to come inside with him. Officer Miller arrived, saw Smith's refusals, and requested more help including a canine unit. Officer Quinn arrived with the police dog Quando, and Officer Medina also responded. Quinn told Smith to turn around and put his hands on his head. Smith refused, although he was told the dog could be sent and might bite. Without further warning Quinn sprayed Smith in the face with pepper spray. Smith swore and tried to go back inside, but his wife had locked the door. Several officers moved onto the porch, grabbed Smith from behind, slammed him against the door, and threw him down on the porch, and Quinn ordered the dog to attack. Quando bit Smith on the right shoulder and neck area, and at some point sank his teeth into Smith's arm. With at least four officers around him and the dog's teeth in his shoulder and neck, Smith agreed to comply and submit to arrest. He admitted he was curled up in a fetal position to shield himself from the dog and that one hand was tucked out of the officers' view. As an officer tried to secure both arms, Quinn had the dog bite Smith a second time, on the left side and shoulder blade. The dog was called off and officers dragged Smith off the porch face down. Smith continued to shield one arm, and Quinn ordered a third bite, to the buttock. Smith was pepper sprayed at least four times in all, at least twice after the dog had broken his skin and at least once after officers had him pinned. Officers handcuffed him, and Reinbolt washed his eyes with a hose but did not clean the bite wounds. Paramedics arrived and treated him. Smith pleaded guilty in state court to violating Penal Code section 148(a)(1), resisting, delaying, or obstructing a peace officer, and received 36 months probation. He also pleaded guilty to spousal battery, which was not at issue. He sued under section 1983, alleging excessive force in the pepper spray and the dog. The district court granted summary judgment on the ground that Heck versus Humphrey barred the suit, and the Ninth Circuit took the appeal en banc.

The holding. Reversed and remanded. The en banc court addressed three questions. Heck versus Humphrey. Under Heck, a section 1983 claim is barred if success would necessarily imply the invalidity of an outstanding conviction. In California, the lawfulness of the officer's conduct is an element of section 148(a)(1), and an arrest made with excessive force is unlawful, so a person cannot be convicted under section 148 for resisting an arrest effected with excessive force. But "the time of the arrest" does not include earlier stages such as an investigation. A conviction based on conduct that occurred before officers began arresting the defendant is not necessarily invalidated by excessive force used later in making the arrest, and excessive force after an arrest may likewise be the subject of a suit. Here the parties agreed Smith violated section 148 several times while standing alone on his porch during the investigative phase, by refusing to show his hands, going inside, and refusing to put his hands on his head. He also resisted after officers came onto the porch. Nothing in the record showed which conduct was the factual basis of his plea. If the plea rested on his resistance during the arrest, Heck would bar the suit; if it rested on his pre-arrest obstruction, it would not. Because the court could not tell, success would not necessarily impugn the conviction, and Heck did not bar the suit. In a footnote the court placed the burden on the defendants to show that success would necessarily imply invalidity, and distinguished the California Court of Appeal's Susag decision as involving a jury verdict rather than a plea and as misapplying federal law. Excessive force. Applying Graham, the court first assessed the quantum of force. By the defendants' own account the force was severe: Hemet's use of force policy classified both pepper spray and a police dog as intermediate force, the most severe force authorized short of deadly force. On Smith's account the force was greater still: four sprayings, one while pinned; slammed against the wall and thrown down; dragged off the porch face down; three dog attacks, at least one while pinned; and pepper spray left in the bite wounds. On the most important Graham factor, immediate threat, the record gave no basis to believe Smith was armed or dangerous; his wife had said there were no weapons and he was in pajamas, he eventually showed his hands before any force was used, and Officer Quinn said Smith made no threats. The crime, domestic abuse, was serious but the circumstances did not show a particularly dangerous offender. Smith ignored commands and briefly went inside but did not flee or attack anyone, and his physical resistance was brief. The availability of alternatives is also a factor, and Smith's expert, relying on California POST standards, said officers could and should have used control holds once Smith was on the ground rather than the dog. "In sum, Smith has submitted a substantial amount of evidence from which a reasonable jury could conclude that the force used against him was excessive." The court left qualified immunity for the district court, noting in a footnote that under Mendoza versus Block and LaLonde versus County of Riverside the use of a dog or pepper spray on a surrendered, controlled arrestee could constitute excessive force under clearly established law. Deadly force. Garner permits deadly force only when necessary to prevent escape and the officer has probable cause to believe the suspect poses a significant threat of death or serious physical injury. The Supreme Court did not define deadly force. In Vera Cruz the Ninth Circuit had defined it as force reasonably likely to kill, refusing to add serious bodily injury or to use substantial risk. The en banc court held that definition "incorrect" and "unduly restrictive," noted that seven other circuits used the definition derived from the Model Penal Code, and observed that a substantial risk of serious bodily injury definition is used by police in all fifty states, including California and the Hemet Police Department, without the difficulties Vera Cruz feared. Using only the objective part of the Model Penal Code test, the court held: "We define deadly force as force that creates a substantial risk of causing death or serious bodily injury." Vera Cruz was overruled. The dog. "We need not here determine whether the use of a police dog to subdue a suspect constitutes deadly force generally or the circumstances under which such use might constitute such force." The court left the district court to apply the new definition first. It noted that it had never found a police dog to be deadly force but had never said it could not be. The dissent. Judge Silverman, joined by Judges Kleinfeld and Callahan, would have held the suit barred by Heck. In his view the encounter was one continuous five minute sequence culminating in one arrest, a California section 148 conviction establishes that the force used to effect the arrest was not excessive, and California's continuous course of conduct rule means a single conviction covers all the acts of resistance. He relied on Susag versus City of Lake Forest. He did not reach the force questions, but noted in a footnote that the dog's teeth had been capped, that the bites were superficial, and that Smith needed no treatment beyond evaluation at the hospital.

On the dissent. Judge Silverman, joined by Judges Kleinfeld and Callahan, dissented on the Heck question. He wrote that from the moment officers told Smith to take his hands out of his pockets until he was handcuffed, the police were trying to detain him and he resisted, in one continuous, uninterrupted sequence of about five minutes that culminated in one arrest. Under California law, he said, a conviction for resisting arrest establishes that the force used to effect the arrest was not excessive, and under California's continuous course of conduct rule a single section 148 conviction covers all acts of resistance that are part of the same transaction. He contrasted Sanford versus Motts, where the plaintiff was punched after being handcuffed, and relied on Susag versus City of Lake Forest, where the California Court of Appeal held a section 148 conviction barred a suit over pepper spray used before the plaintiff was subdued. He said the majority was not free to substitute its view of California law for the Court of Appeal's. Because he found the suit barred, he did not reach the force questions, but added that the dog's teeth had been capped and could not inflict deep punctures, that the bites were superficial and cleaned at the scene, and that Smith was evaluated at a hospital and needed no further treatment.

What was not decided. Whether a police dog is deadly force, either generally or on these facts. The court expressly left that question to the district court to decide first under the new definition. Whether the officers actually used excessive force. The court held only that a reasonable jury could so find on Smith's version of the facts. Qualified immunity. The court declined to resolve it and left it for the district court. Whether Smith's plea in fact rested on pre-arrest or arrest-phase conduct. The court could not tell from the record and ruled only that the uncertainty defeated the Heck bar at summary judgment. Whether pepper spray or a police dog is intermediate force as a matter of constitutional law. The court used the Hemet Police Department's own classification as a starting point for assessing the quantum of force. Whether Vera Cruz was correct about the dog in that case. The court overruled its definition of deadly force; it did not revisit the facts of Vera Cruz.

What this means in practice. Smith versus City of Hemet gives California officers the Ninth Circuit's definition of deadly force: force that creates a substantial risk of causing death or serious bodily injury. The court adopted that language because California and the Hemet Police Department already used it, so the constitutional definition and the state definition now say the same thing. The practical effect is that the question of whether a tool or technique is deadly force turns on the risk it creates in the way it is used, not on whether the tool is a firearm. The court did not say a police dog is deadly force. It said it would leave that to the district court. Teaching Smith as a holding that K-9 bites are deadly force, or that the dog deployment in Smith was found reasonable, misstates the case. What the court did hold is that pepper spray and a dog, classified by the department itself as intermediate force, applied repeatedly to a man in pajamas who had shown his hands, made no threats, and was pinned by four officers, could be found excessive by a jury. The Heck ruling matters for report writing and for understanding section 148. In California an arrest made with excessive force is not a lawful performance of duty, so a section 148 conviction based on resistance during such an arrest cannot stand with an excessive force finding. But obstruction during the investigation, before officers decided to arrest, is a separate matter, and a plea that does not say which conduct it covers does not protect officers from suit. Reports should distinguish the investigative phase from the arrest, state when the decision to arrest was made, and describe each application of force and the resistance that preceded it. I teach Smith for the deadly force definition and its match with Penal Code section 835 a, for the lesson that dogs and other tools can become deadly force depending on how they are used, and for the principle that force must match the moment: when resistance stops, the justification stops.

How to train it. Teach the definition and make students apply it to tools, not labels. Give the Smith definition, force that creates a substantial risk of causing death or serious bodily injury, and run a list: baton strike to the head, baton strike to the thigh, impact munition at the chest from thirty feet, carotid restraint, dog bite to the arm, dog bite to the neck. For each, students explain what risk the application creates and why that makes it deadly force or not. The point is that the same tool can fall on either side depending on target, duration, and circumstances. Teach the stop signal. The force in Smith continued after he said he would comply and after four officers had him pinned: a second bite while an officer secured his arms, a third bite after he was dragged off the porch, pepper spray while pinned. Build arrest and control evolutions where the role player resists, then announces compliance while still partially curled, and require students to transition from force to control holds and to call the dog or spray off. Smith's expert said control holds were the available alternative once he was on the ground; students should show that transition. Teach the two phases for reports. Have students write the Smith encounter as a report that separates the investigation (commands from the yard, refusals, going inside) from the arrest (the decision to go onto the porch and each application of force), with the resistance that preceded each application. Explain that in California a section 148 conviction depends on the officer acting lawfully at the time of the resistance, and that excessive force in the arrest can defeat a conviction for resisting that arrest. Teach the dog and spray honestly. Hemet classified both as intermediate force. Warn before using either when feasible, direct the dog to a specific target and purpose, release on compliance, and decontaminate wounds and eyes. The court noted the bite wounds were not cleaned of pepper spray. Teach the posture: an en banc reversal of summary judgment, a new definition of deadly force, the dog question left open, qualified immunity left open, and a three judge dissent on the Heck issue.

The briefing room points, the part to take into a briefing or a training block.

First, The definition. Deadly force is force that creates a substantial risk of causing death or serious bodily injury. The Ninth Circuit adopted it because California agencies already used it. Apply it to how a tool is used, not to the name of the tool.

Second, The court did not call the dog deadly force. The en banc court expressly declined to decide whether a police dog is deadly force, generally or on these facts, and left that to the district court. Course materials that say Smith held K-9 bites are deadly force, or that the court found the deployment reasonable, overstate the case.

Third, When compliance starts, force must change. A jury could find excessive force where a man who had shown his hands, made no threats, and was pinned by four officers was bitten a second and third time and sprayed again. The expert said control holds were the available alternative once he was down. Transition to control.

Fourth, Investigation and arrest are different phases. Obstruction while officers investigate from the yard is not erased by excessive force used later during the arrest, but a section 148 plea that does not say which conduct it covers does not bar the suit. Reports should mark when the arrest began and describe each application of force and the resistance before it.

Fifth, Decontaminate everything. Officers flushed Smith's eyes but not the dog bite wounds, and the court noted that the pepper spray in the wounds made the force worse. Aftercare is part of the force.
