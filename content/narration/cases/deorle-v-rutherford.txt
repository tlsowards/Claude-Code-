Deorle versus Rutherford. From the Sowards Briefing Room. A SIRT deputy fired a lead-filled beanbag round into the face of an unarmed, suicidal man who was walking toward him at a steady gait on his own property, after the man had obeyed commands to drop a crossbow and other items, while negotiators were still en route. The deputy gave no warning and no order to stop. The Ninth Circuit reversed summary judgment, holding that on the plaintiff's version the force was excessive and that the deputy was not entitled to qualified immunity. The court held that force capable of causing serious injury, though less than deadly, may be used only when a strong governmental interest warrants it, that an officer's bare statement of fear is not enough, that the subject's apparent emotional disturbance is a factor, and that a warning should be given when feasible. It is the foundation of the less lethal line.

Deorle versus Rutherford. Ninth Circuit, decided March 16, 2001; amended August 31, 2001; second amended opinion filed November 19, 2001, docket number 99-17188. This is a published decision.

Here are the facts. The facts are the record viewed in the light most favorable to Deorle, as the court was required to view it on review of summary judgment. On September 9, 1996, in Butte County, California, Richard Deorle, upset at a diagnosis of hepatitis C and having consumed a half pint of vodka and his prescribed Interferon, began behaving erratically and became suicidal. His wife called 911 for help. Officer Mahon responded; Mrs. Deorle and the children left the house without interference from Deorle, who angrily refused to let Mahon enter without a warrant. Mahon radioed for Code 3 backup. At least thirteen officers responded, set up roadblocks on the streets around the house so Deorle could not leave, and waited for a Special Incident Response Team and a team of negotiators. The department's manual described negotiators as essential to resolving critical incidents. Deorle was verbally abusive but physically compliant and generally followed officers' instructions. When a canine team made a police dog bark aggressively at him to test his behavior, he retreated toward his house. When a porch board came off in his hands, he dropped it on command. Although he shouted "kill me" and brandished a hatchet at an officer, he threw the hatchet into a clump of trees when told to put it down. He continued to roam on or near his property within the roadblocks. Deputy Rutherford, on scene for thirty to forty minutes, did not see Deorle touch or attack anyone and received no report that he had. He did hear Deorle scream that he would kick his ass. Rutherford, a SIRT member trained in the use of force against recalcitrant suspects, decided after a briefing to move closer to reconnoiter, although the negotiators had not arrived. With two other officers, he watched Deorle from the cover of trees for five to ten minutes. Deorle, carrying an unloaded plastic crossbow in one hand and what may have been a can or bottle of lighter fluid in the other, started shouting at the officers. Rutherford carried a twelve gauge shotgun loaded with a lead-filled cloth-cased round, which by his own admission could be lethal at thirty feet and potentially lethal up to fifty feet, with the face as the probable lethal target area. Rutherford shouted at Deorle to put down the crossbow and Deorle discarded it. Deorle then walked directly toward Rutherford at a steady gait, not running. Rutherford widened his stance, braced his weapon against a tree, aimed at the lower right rib area, and waited until Deorle reached a point he had predetermined, then fired from about thirty feet. He did not warn Deorle that he would be shot, did not order him to stop, and did not tell him to drop the can or bottle. The round struck Deorle in the face, knocked him down, and lodged half out of his eye. Deorle suffered multiple skull fractures, lost his left eye, and had lead shot embedded in his skull. Negotiators were still en route. Deorle was wearing only cut-off shorts. He was ultimately charged with nothing more than obstructing officers under California Penal Code section 69. Deorle sued Rutherford, the sheriff, the county, and the round's manufacturer. The district court held Rutherford was entitled to qualified immunity and did not violate Deorle's rights, granted summary judgment to the defendants, and denied reconsideration. The only issue on appeal was the excessive force claim against Rutherford as it reached the court through the qualified immunity ruling.

The holding. Reversed and remanded. Following Saucier versus Katz, the court first asked whether the facts, viewed for the plaintiff, showed a constitutional violation, and then whether the right was clearly established. The quantum of force. The court assessed the type and amount of force first. The cloth-cased shot is a long range impact weapon that, by Rutherford's own admission, was potentially lethal at thirty feet and at the face. It is much greater force than pepper spray or a pain compliance hold and more likely to cause life threatening injury than most dog bites. It fell short of deadly force as then defined in the circuit under Vera Cruz versus City of Escondido (force reasonably likely to cause death), but it "constitutes force which has the capability of causing serious injury." The court stated the rule: "the degree of force used by Rutherford is permissible only when a strong governmental interest compels the employment of such force." A footnote called the beanbag label a euphemism that grossly underrates the projectile's dangerousness. The governmental interest. The court evaluated the severity of the crime, the immediate threat, active resistance or flight, and other exigencies, calling them means of determining objectively the amount of force necessary. Officers came to investigate peculiar behavior, not to arrest. Deorle was plainly emotionally disturbed and suicidal. The crime being committed, if any, was minor. Rutherford had watched Deorle for five to ten minutes at close range, had seen him discard the crossbow on command, and knew he carried only a can or bottle. "A desire to resolve quickly a potentially dangerous situation is not the type of governmental interest that, standing alone, justifies the use of force that may cause serious injury." And "a simple statement by an officer that he fears for his safety or the safety of others is not enough; there must be objective factors to justify such a concern." The objective facts: roadblocks staffed by officers, no attack or attempt to harm anyone in forty minutes, no flight, negotiators on the way, a secure position behind a tree with a clear line of retreat and another officer behind him, no bystanders nearby, and no evidence Rutherford considered less dangerous methods. The governmental interest in using force capable of causing serious injury "was clearly not substantial." Weighing. Given the number of officers, the pending arrival of negotiators, the minor nature of the conduct, Deorle's compliance with prior commands, the absence of any assault, and his having discarded the crossbow and being unarmed when fired upon, the fact that he was walking toward Rutherford on his own property with a can or bottle in his hand "is insufficient by any objective measure to justify the force deployed." The court stressed the absence of any warning or command: Deorle "certainly could not have been expected to comply with instructions that were never given to him." Emotional disturbance. The tactics appropriate for an unarmed, emotionally distraught person creating a disturbance ordinarily differ from those for an armed and dangerous criminal; escalating force may make the first situation worse, and the use of officers trained in crisis negotiation is ordinarily advisable where feasible. The court did not adopt a per se rule with two classes of suspects, but held that "where it is or should be apparent to the officers that the individual involved is emotionally disturbed, that is a factor that must be considered in determining, under Graham, the reasonableness of the force employed." Split second judgments. The court acknowledged Graham's allowance for split second decisions but found this was not that situation: officers had been on scene more than half an hour, Rutherford had observed Deorle at length, had consulted superiors, had a line of retreat and a roadblock behind him, and "made a calculated and deliberate decision to shoot" when Deorle reached a chosen point. Warnings. Officers in the court's cases gave warnings where feasible even for less than deadly force. "We do not hold, however, that warnings are required whenever less than deadly force is employed. Rather, we simply determine that such warnings should be given, when feasible, if the use of force may result in serious injury, and that the giving of a warning or the failure to do so is a factor to be considered in applying the Graham balancing test." Here a warning was obviously desirable and feasible and there was ample time. A shout of "less lethal," even if made before the shot, was not a warning to the target. The rule stated. Less than deadly force, like deadly force, is subject to Graham balancing. "Less than deadly force that may lead to serious injury may be used only when a strong governmental interest warrants its use, and in such circumstances should be preceded by a warning, when feasible." On the plaintiff's version, the shooting violated the Fourth Amendment. Qualified immunity. This was "by no means a borderline case." Every officer should know it is objectively unreasonable to shoot, even with lead shot in a cloth case, an unarmed man who has committed no serious offense, is emotionally disturbed, has been given no warning, poses no risk of flight, and presents no objectively reasonable threat. The absence of a prior case on these precise facts did not entitle Rutherford to immunity; under Anderson versus Creighton and Mendoza versus Block, closely analogous precedent is not required when the conduct is patently unconstitutional, and an officer does not get immunity every time a novel method is used to inflict injury. Viewing the facts for the plaintiff, Rutherford was not entitled to qualified immunity.

On the dissent. Judge Silverman dissented. He stressed facts he considered undisputed: Deorle had earlier swung or held a board with protruding nails, picked up two hatchets and a crossbow, said he would kill anyone who came onto his property, told his wife the police would have to kill him, and advanced on Rutherford holding a quart can of lighter fluid while threatening to kick his ass. He wrote that Rutherford had no duty to wait to be doused with a flammable liquid, that the majority was wrong to call Deorle unarmed, that the round was designed to incapacitate rather than injure, that firing at center mass from twenty to forty feet was not reasonably likely to cause death or serious injury, and that the round unexpectedly flew up and struck the face. On the second Saucier step he argued no case established a clearly established rule against what Rutherford did, that the pain compliance and house entry cases the majority cited were not on point, and that the cases involving warnings were decided after the incident. He also noted that the plaintiff's expert declaration said nothing about any experience with beanbag rounds. He would have affirmed summary judgment for Rutherford and the sheriff.

What was not decided. Whether Rutherford actually used excessive force. The court decided only that, viewing the facts in Deorle's favor on summary judgment, the force was excessive and immunity was unavailable. The case was remanded for further proceedings. Liability of the sheriff, the county, or the manufacturer. Only the excessive force claim against Rutherford, through the qualified immunity ruling, was before the court. Whether a beanbag round is deadly force. The court applied the then controlling Vera Cruz definition and said the question was not whether the force was deadly but whether it was justified. The Ninth Circuit later changed the definition of deadly force in Smith versus City of Hemet (2005). Whether warnings are always required before less than deadly force. The court expressly declined to so hold, ruling that a warning should be given when feasible if the force may cause serious injury, and that giving or failing to give one is a factor. A per se rule for mentally ill subjects. The court declined to create two categories of suspects and held only that apparent emotional disturbance is a factor.

What this means in practice. Deorle sets the standard for every impact munition, and by later extension for other intermediate tools: if the force can cause serious injury, it requires a strong governmental interest, and a warning should precede it when feasible. The officer's job is to be able to name the interest and the warning. Name the interest in objective facts. The court rejected a bare statement of fear. What counts is what the officer saw and knew: a weapon in hand, an attack or attempt to attack, a flight risk, bystanders at risk, a serious crime. In Deorle the record showed the opposite on every point, plus containment, numbers, and negotiators minutes away. Give the warning the target can act on. Rutherford waited for Deorle to cross an undisclosed line. The court said Deorle could not comply with instructions never given. Shouting "less lethal" to other officers is not a warning. Tell the person to stop, tell him what to drop, and tell him what will happen if he does not. Time changes the analysis. Graham's split second allowance did not apply to an officer who had watched the subject for minutes, had cover, had a retreat path, and chose his firing point in advance. When time exists, the court expects it to be used for alternatives and warnings. Apparent mental crisis is part of the calculus. It is not a shield that forbids force, and the court said so, but it is a factor the officer must weigh, and it points toward negotiation and time where feasible. I teach Deorle as the first case in the less lethal line, with the training points that less lethal is not no risk, and that officers must warn and justify before deploying impact munitions. I also connect Deorle's treatment of emotional disturbance to California's statutory direction to consider a person's apparent mental health disability when evaluating force.

How to train it. Teach the Deorle articulation before teaching the shot. Before any impact munition training evolution, require the student to state three things aloud: the objective facts showing an immediate threat or other strong interest, the warning given and the time allowed for compliance, and the alternatives available and why they were not used. The court faulted the record for lacking all three. Build the warning into muscle memory. The standard the opinion implies is a command to stop, a command to drop any object, and a statement that the person will be shot with a less lethal round if he does not comply, given when feasible, with time to comply. Train the warning as part of the deployment, and train the announcement to other officers as a separate act that does not substitute for it. Teach the tool honestly. The deputy admitted the round could be lethal at thirty feet, that the face is the lethal target area, and that he aimed at the lower right ribs from about thirty feet and struck the face. Students must know the manufacturer's stated ranges, the lethal zones, the realistic dispersion of a round fired from a shotgun, and the court's footnote that rounds fired from shotguns are often off the mark. Target selection and distance are part of reasonableness. Train the time decision. Run scenarios where the subject is contained, numbers are on scene, and a negotiator is minutes away. Students must decide whether to act or hold and must defend the choice in Graham terms. Deorle teaches that a predetermined decision to fire when a subject crosses an unannounced line is a calculated decision that will be judged as one. Teach the mental health factor as a factor. Students should be able to say that apparent emotional disturbance lowers the governmental interest and changes the tactics, without claiming the law forbids force on a person in crisis. Teach the posture: summary judgment reversed on the plaintiff's version of the facts, with a dissent, and the case remanded. The court did not try the case.

The briefing room points, the part to take into a briefing or a training block.

First, Serious injury force needs a strong interest. The court held that force capable of causing serious injury, even though less than deadly, is permissible only when a strong governmental interest compels it. Before deploying an impact munition, be able to state that interest in objective facts.

Second, Fear is not a fact. A simple statement that the officer feared for his safety is not enough. There must be objective factors to justify the concern. What did the person have, what did he do, who was at risk, and what did you actually see.

Third, Warn when feasible, and warn the target. Warnings should be given when feasible if the force may result in serious injury, and the giving or failure to give one is a Graham factor. An announcement of less lethal to other officers is not a warning to the subject. Order him to stop and tell him what is coming.

Fourth, Time removes the split second defense. Officers were on scene over half an hour, Rutherford watched Deorle for five to ten minutes, had cover and a retreat path, and chose his firing point in advance. The court called the shot a calculated and deliberate decision, not a split second judgment.

Fifth, Apparent crisis is a factor, not a rule. The court declined to create a separate class of mentally ill suspects, but held that where it is or should be apparent that the person is emotionally disturbed, that fact must be considered in weighing the force. It favors negotiators and time where feasible.

Sixth, Posture. Summary judgment for the deputy was reversed on the plaintiff's version of the facts and the case was remanded. The court did not try the case or enter judgment against anyone, and Judge Silverman dissented.
