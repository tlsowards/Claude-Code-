Bryan versus MacPherson. From the Sowards Briefing Room. A Coronado officer fired a Taser X26 in dart mode, without warning, at a shirtless, unarmed 21 year old who was standing fifteen to twenty-five feet away, yelling gibberish and hitting his own thighs after a seatbelt stop. The Ninth Circuit held that a Taser in dart mode is an intermediate, significant level of force that must be justified by the governmental interest involved, that on the plaintiff's version the force was excessive, and that the officer nonetheless had qualified immunity because no controlling decision had addressed dart mode Tasers before July 2005. It is the Ninth Circuit's foundational Taser case.

Bryan versus MacPherson. Ninth Circuit, decided November 30, 2010, docket number 08-55622. This is a published decision.

Here are the facts. The facts are Bryan's version, as the court was required to view them on an interlocutory appeal from the denial of summary judgment. On the morning of July 24, 2005, Carl Bryan, age 21, was driving his younger brother home to Coronado after a bad start to his day: a lost set of keys, a long detour, and a speeding ticket from the California Highway Patrol that left him crying and moping. He had taken off his t-shirt to wipe his face and was wearing only boxer shorts and tennis shoes. At about 7:30 a.m., stopped at an intersection, he was signaled to stop by Officer Brian MacPherson, who was enforcing seatbelt regulations. Bryan realized he had forgotten to re-buckle. MacPherson asked whether he knew why he had been stopped; Bryan stared ahead. MacPherson asked him to turn down the radio and pull to the curb, and Bryan did both, hitting his steering wheel and yelling expletives at himself as he did. He parked and stepped out. It was undisputed that Bryan was agitated, standing outside his car, yelling gibberish and hitting his own thighs, that he did not verbally threaten the officer, that he was, by the officer's own account, twenty to twenty-five feet away, and that he was not trying to flee. MacPherson testified that he told Bryan to stay in the car; Bryan testified that he did not hear that command. The one material dispute was movement: MacPherson said Bryan took one step toward him, Bryan denied taking any step, and the physical evidence (the probe lodged in the side of his arm, the blood pattern on the pavement, and both men's testimony that he fell forward) indicated he was facing away from the officer. Without warning, MacPherson shot Bryan with a Taser X26 in dart mode. One probe embedded in the side of his upper left arm. The current immobilized him and he fell face first onto the asphalt, fracturing four teeth and suffering facial contusions. A probe had to be removed with a scalpel at a hospital. Bryan was arrested and charged with resisting an officer under Penal Code section 148; a jury hung and the charge was dismissed. Bryan sued MacPherson, the police department, the chief, and the city for excessive force, state torts, and failure to train. The district court granted summary judgment to the city and department but denied MacPherson qualified immunity, finding that a jury could conclude Bryan presented no immediate danger and that a reasonable officer would have known the shot would cause pain and a fall onto asphalt. MacPherson took an interlocutory appeal.

The holding. The court held that, viewing the facts for Bryan, the Taser deployment was constitutionally excessive, but that the right was not clearly established on July 24, 2005, so MacPherson was entitled to qualified immunity. The denial of summary judgment was reversed. The quantum of force. The court first assessed the type and amount of force. The X26 propels barbed probes at over 160 feet per second and delivers a 1200 volt, low ampere charge that overrides the central nervous system, paralyzes the muscles throughout the body, and causes intense pain felt throughout the body, followed by disorientation, loss of balance and weakness, and the foreseeable risk of injury from an uncontrolled fall. Tasers are non-lethal, but "non-lethal" is not the same as non-excessive, and non-lethal is not one category: pepper spray and baton blows are not constitutionally equivalent simply because both are non-lethal. The court compared the Taser to pepper spray in Headwaters and to the nunchaku pain compliance in Forrester and found it a greater intrusion than either: the pain is more intense, not localized, not gradual, and not within the subject's control. It held that "the X26 and similar devices when used in dart-mode constitute an intermediate, significant level of force that must be justified by the governmental interest involved." A footnote extends the holding to all controlled electric devices that cause similar physiological effects. The court also recognized the Taser's value in defusing dangerous situations from a distance. The governmental interest. Immediate threat is the most important factor, and a simple statement that the officer feared for his safety is not enough. Bryan was unarmed and obviously so, made no physical or verbal threat, stood fifteen to twenty-five feet away without advancing, and was facing away. Even one step would have left him nineteen to twenty-four feet away. There were no bystanders in danger. MacPherson had his X26 drawn and charged, ready to respond to any change. At most he faced a disturbed and upset young man, not an immediately threatening one. The court distinguished the Eleventh Circuit's Draper decision, where a belligerent driver argued with the officer and paced within feet of him. Severity: a seatbelt infraction, plus suspected non-violent misdemeanors, provides little basis for significant force; traffic violations generally will not support it. If MacPherson believed Bryan was mentally disturbed, that cut against force, not for it: the government's interest in using force to detain a non-threatening mentally ill person is less than its interest in apprehending a dangerous criminal, and the purpose is to help, not punish. Resistance: Bryan complied with every command except the one he says he did not hear. Failing to stay in the car is not active resistance. Resistance is not binary; it runs from the purely passive protester to the person assaulting the officer, and the court placed Bryan's conduct closer to the passive end, or, on his version, not resistance at all. Two further factors weighed against the officer: he gave no warning that he would fire if Bryan did not comply, although there was time and no reason not to, and he did not consider less intrusive alternatives when he knew backup was en route. The court stated that officers need not use the least intrusive force, but must consider less intrusive methods, and the presence of feasible alternatives is a factor. Balancing. The government had at best a minimal interest, insufficient to justify intermediate force. The situation was tense but static, with the officer ready to respond while awaiting backup. The officer's desire to end an unusual situation quickly and decisively was understandable, but his chosen method violated the Fourth Amendment. Qualified immunity. Graham, Deorle and Headwaters gave fair notice that intermediate force was unjustified on these facts, but as of July 2005 no Supreme Court or Ninth Circuit decision had addressed whether a Taser in dart mode was intermediate force; the circuit's only published reference had called Tasers pain compliance weapons, and two other panels had found Taser law not clearly established. A reasonable officer could have made a reasonable mistake of law, so MacPherson was immune.

On the dissent. Judge Tallman, joined by Judges Callahan and N.R. Smith, dissented from the denial of rehearing en banc. He wrote that MacPherson, alone, faced a mostly naked man who reacted with irrational rage to a stop, ignored commands, and did not retreat, and that any reasonable officer would be concerned for his safety. He argued that the panel related the facts from Bryan's perspective rather than the officer's, that Deorle's requirement of a strong governmental interest that compels the force is not the Supreme Court's standard and that the panel's one sentence amendment did not cure its reliance on Deorle, and that the panel used a contested summary judgment record to make sweeping findings about Tasers that would limit their use throughout the circuit. He agreed the officer was entitled to qualified immunity and would have stopped there. Judge Wardlaw, joined by Judges Pregerson, Reinhardt and W. Fletcher, concurred in the denial, answering that the intermediate force conclusion was within the national mainstream of judicial decisions and professional literature and that the record showed the officer faced an unarmed, stationary, non-threatening man.

What was not decided. Whether MacPherson is liable. The court decided only that on Bryan's version of the facts the force was excessive and that qualified immunity nonetheless applied because the law was not clearly established in 2005. There was no trial and no verdict. The level of force of a Taser in drive stun mode. The holding is limited to dart mode and devices with similar physiological effects. The en banc court in Mattos later declined to decide the drive stun question on the record before it. Whether the Eleventh Circuit's Draper decision is the law of the Ninth Circuit. The court expressly did not adopt it and distinguished it on its facts. Whether warnings are always required before a Taser is used. Following Deorle, the failure to warn when feasible is a factor in the balance, not a separate rule. Claims against the city and department. Summary judgment for them was not before the court.

What this means in practice. Bryan tells the officer three things about the Taser. First, a dart mode deployment is intermediate force, not a shortcut around the force analysis. It must be justified by the same Graham interests as a baton or an impact munition, and the court placed it above pepper spray and pain compliance holds on the scale of intrusion. Second, the justification must be objective. Bizarre, loud, non-compliant behavior by a visibly unarmed person twenty feet away did not make him an immediate threat, and the court would not accept the officer's fear without objective facts to back it. Third, time and alternatives matter. A static scene with backup minutes away is not the split second situation Graham protects; the court expected the officer to warn and to consider other options before firing. The mental health point cuts the opposite way from how some officers think of it. The officer argued the Taser was justified because Bryan might be mentally ill. The court answered that a belief the subject is disturbed calls for more effort at less intrusive control, because the government's interest in forcing a non-threatening person in crisis into custody is lower, not higher. The immunity outcome should not be read as approval. The court held that the shot was unconstitutional on Bryan's facts and granted immunity only because Taser law was undeveloped in 2005. After Bryan, Mattos and Brooks, that gap is closed in the Ninth Circuit. I teach Bryan in the less lethal line with Deorle, Mattos and Brooks, and Young: name the level of force, name the interest that justifies it, warn when feasible, and distinguish passive from active resistance.

How to train it. Teach the classification first. Students should be able to state that a conducted energy weapon in dart mode is intermediate force in the Ninth Circuit and to explain why the court said so: neuromuscular incapacitation, whole body pain, and the foreseeable fall. A deployment lesson that treats the Taser as a compliance tool for any non-compliance will not survive this case. Build the threat articulation around objective facts. Run a scenario with a half dressed, agitated subject who is yelling but stationary and unarmed. Require the student to state distance, what the subject's hands are doing, what the subject has, whether anyone else is at risk, and what the subject has actually done. Then ask whether the situation is static or evolving and whether backup is coming. Bryan teaches that "he was acting bizarre" is not an articulation. Train the warning as part of the deployment. The officer's repeated command to stay in the car was a command, not a warning that the Taser would be used. Students should give the command, state the consequence, and allow time to comply when feasible, and should be able to explain in a report why a warning was not feasible when it was not given. Teach the resistance spectrum. Use the court's language that resistance runs from the purely passive protester to the person assaulting the officer, and have students place real scenarios on that line and match the force option to the position. Failure to follow a single command sits near the passive end. Teach the posture honestly. The district court denied immunity, the Ninth Circuit found a violation on the plaintiff's facts, and immunity was granted only because the law was unsettled in 2005. Students should understand that the same deployment today would not get the benefit of that doubt.

The briefing room points, the part to take into a briefing or a training block.

First, Dart mode is intermediate force. The court held that the X26 and similar devices in dart mode are an intermediate, significant level of force that must be justified by the governmental interest involved. Treat a probe deployment the way you would treat a baton: it needs a reason of matching weight.

Second, Non-lethal is not non-excessive. All force, lethal and non-lethal, must be justified by the need for that specific level of force. Pepper spray, a baton and a Taser are not interchangeable because they share a label. Articulate the tool you used and why that tool.

Third, Objective facts, not adjectives. A visibly unarmed man fifteen to twenty-five feet away, yelling gibberish and facing away, was not an immediate threat. The court said a Fourth Amendment violation does not depend on whether the officer can find enough compelling adjectives for the subject's conduct.

Fourth, Apparent crisis lowers the interest. If the officer believed Bryan was mentally disturbed, the court said he should have made greater effort at less intrusive means. The interest in forcing a non-threatening person in crisis into custody is less than the interest in seizing a dangerous criminal.

Fifth, Warn and consider alternatives when time allows. There was time to warn that the Taser would be fired and no reason not to. Backup was en route. The failure to warn and the failure to consider less intrusive means factored significantly into the court's balance, even though officers need not choose the least intrusive option.

Sixth, Posture. The court found a constitutional violation on Bryan's version of the facts but granted qualified immunity because no controlling case had classified dart mode Tasers before July 2005. There was no trial. The immunity ruling is about the state of the law then, not approval of the shot.
