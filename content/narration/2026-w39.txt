Now, the Ninth Circuit. One published opinion, described in the episode as the most consequential Ninth Circuit use of force decision in some time. It is a merits holding, not a clearly established dodge, which makes it a decision training can be built around.

Handy versus Jones. Ninth Circuit (from the District of Alaska), decided September 15, 2026. This is a published decision. A verification note before the facts. Judge Callahan wrote the opinion; the other two panel names were garbled in the source and are omitted here. No docket number was given. Here are the facts. Late at night, Anchorage police responded to reports of a domestic disturbance involving Christopher Handy and his girlfriend. Dispatch told the responding officers three things: Handy had assaulted his girlfriend, he may have discharged a firearm, and he was outside his apartment armed with a shotgun waiting for the police. Officers arrived, announced themselves, and ordered Handy to come out and drop the weapon. He came out carrying the shotgun. He refused repeated instructions to disarm. He advanced toward the officers, waving the shotgun over his head and cursing at them. As he stepped off the curb, officers fired, and he died. His relatives sued under the Fourth Amendment for excessive force and for deprivation of the familial relationship. The district court in Alaska denied the officers qualified immunity at the pleading stage. The judge relied on a line of Ninth Circuit precedent suggesting that an officer may not use deadly force against an armed suspect unless the suspect makes some threatening gesture, and found that disputes about exactly what Handy did with the shotgun meant the case could not be resolved on a motion to dismiss. Video of the encounter had been incorporated by reference into the complaint itself. The holding. Vacated, with instructions to grant qualified immunity, in an opinion by Judge Callahan. The court considered the totality of the circumstances, including the video evidence incorporated into the complaint, and held that the officers acted reasonably and did not violate anyone's federal rights. That is a merits holding: the court looked at deadly force used against a man who never pointed the shotgun at anyone and said it was objectively reasonable. In the alternative, the court held that no clearly established law requires officers to hold their fire until an armed suspect actually aims a weapon at them. The district court had essentially treated Ninth Circuit law as containing a wait-to-be-aimed-at rule, and the panel said no such rule exists. Because the recording was clear and part of the complaint, the court was able to decide the case at the pleading stage instead of after two years of discovery and a summary judgment fight. The episode connected the totality analysis to Barnes versus Felix, which last year rejected a narrow moment-of-threat snapshot in favor of the whole sequence. What was not decided. The court did not say that pointing a weapon is irrelevant, and it did not say that an armed man is automatically a lethal threat. The facts included an advance toward officers after refused commands. Strip out the advance and it is a different case; nothing in the opinion says a static armed subject is a lethal threat. What this means in practice. The why matters more than the what. No single fact got the officers there. An assault already reported, a shot possibly already fired, an armed man who positioned himself outside and waited for police rather than fleeing or hiding, commands given and refused, a weapon brandished overhead, and movement toward officers: all of them together made the force reasonable. The dispatch information carried the case, and each of those facts came from the call taker. And a complete, clear recording that captured the approach, the commands, and the subject's response did not just support the defense; it ended the case before discovery. How to train it. Have officers articulate the accumulation in order, out loud: what dispatch said, what they saw, what they ordered, what the subject did, and what changed. Do not let anyone reduce this opinion to a slogan about not having to wait; the advance did the work, and the alternative holding is narrower than it sounds. Run the hypothetical from the episode: a shots-fired call, a woman heard screaming, a man in the front yard holding a rifle at low ready who does not drop it and does not raise it. That is a containment and communication problem, with distance, cover, time, a supervisor, and a negotiator, right up until the rifle starts to come up or he starts to close. Make officers say which specific fact moved them. Train dispatchers that the specificity of what they pass along becomes the legal foundation for what the officer does thirty seconds later; vague is expensive. Review camera policy for what gets preserved, the angle, and the audio, because complete recordings are now a dispositive motion.

Now, the Other federal circuits. Two decisions. The Eleventh Circuit's published opinion in Calloway v. Adcock is a merits win on deadly force against an unarmed subject, described in the episode as the best written use of force opinion of the year. The Fifth Circuit's unpublished per curiam in Rosales v. Lewis is the counterweight: officers lost on both prongs on a stop and frisk, with no force question in the case at all.

Calloway versus Adcock. Eleventh Circuit, decided September 22, 2026. This is a published decision. A verification note before the facts. The transcript renders the case name both as Calloway and as Callaway; the spelling used more often is kept here, but verify it. The panel is given as Judge Ed Carnes, joined by Judges Grant and Abudu, and the opinion is described as 87 pages long. No docket number was given. Here are the facts. Luverne, Alabama, a town of fewer than 3,000 people, evening of May 27, 2020. Channing Spivey, 33, had a grade 4 glioblastoma removed that spring, went through aggressive treatment, then abruptly quit all of his medications. Family described confusion, hollering, incoherent religious talk, and days without sleep. That morning he did donuts in a field and drove his truck into a tree. EMS and Crenshaw County Deputy Brent Penny responded to a combative subject call. Spivey punched through glass, leaving his fists bloody. Penny deployed a Taser; the probes hit, and Spivey pulled them out. A relative ran to the home of Mason Adcock, a captain and assistant chief of the 13-officer Luverne Police Department, who was home with his wife and teenage stepson. Spivey pursued Penny into Adcock's yard. Spivey was 6 feet 3 inches, heavier than Adcock, with a longer reach. Penny never engaged. Adcock's flashlight strike did not slow Spivey down. Spivey landed at least two or three haymakers to Adcock's head, one nearly knocking him unconscious, driving Adcock backward toward his house and his family roughly 20 feet away. Adcock drew, pushed Spivey off, and as Spivey came at him again from about 6 feet, fired all six rounds. Five hit. Spivey died. The district court granted qualified immunity on the clearly established prong. The holding. Affirmed, but on the merits rather than the clearly established prong, in a published opinion by Judge Ed Carnes joined by Judges Grant and Abudu. The court wrote that a reasonable officer in the circumstances this one found himself in could have believed that the use of deadly force was necessary, and that it follows the force was not excessive in violation of the Fourth Amendment. Two doctrinal points. The court expressly declined to decide whether the collective knowledge doctrine applies in excessive force cases, holding it unnecessary, because what Deputy Penny actually communicated to Adcock, by words and by conduct, is part of the totality regardless of any doctrine. And the court measured the officer's confidence in the threat against a probable cause standard, which, as the Supreme Court has said, is not a high bar. The opinion also repeatedly states that the estate's appellate attorney misrepresented the record, took deposition testimony out of context, and in one instance made up a phrase and attributed it to a witness; the court used the word falsehood. What was not decided. Whether the collective knowledge doctrine applies in excessive force cases. The court found it unnecessary to decide because the deputy's actual communications were part of the totality either way. What this means in practice. Unarmed does not mean not deadly. The subject had no weapon, but he was large, enraged, medically deteriorating, impervious to a Taser, and landing full-force head strikes on an officer who was retreating toward his own family. What was behind the officer is the fact that turned the case. The Taser that connected and failed reframed everything that came after it. And a medical crisis and a criminal threat are not mutually exclusive: the court did not hold the subject's condition irrelevant, it held the officer was entitled to respond to what was in front of him. When opposing counsel's brief quotes testimony, pull the transcript; judges do. How to train it. When officers write an unarmed subject report, the narrative has to carry disparity of force, weapon ineffectiveness, the officer's escape route or lack of one, and what was behind the officer. Document device failure in the sentence where it happened, not in a supplemental: the Taser was deployed, it connected, and the subject pulled the probes out. Crisis intervention training should say both halves out loud, that a man off his medications is both a patient and, in that moment, a lethal danger; officers taught only the first half hesitate in the wrong moment, and officers taught only the second half never de-escalate. For small agencies: 13 sworn officers, an off-duty captain at home became the primary, with no supervisor, no backup minutes away, and no less lethal option in his hand. Ask what the plan is when the call comes to an officer's home address, because it happens more than anybody admits.

Rosales versus Lewis. Fifth Circuit, decided September 22, 2026. This decision is unpublished, so it is not binding precedent. A verification note before the facts. Unpublished and described in the episode as short, about six pages. The panel is given as Judges King, Smith, and Ramirez, per curiam. No docket number was given. Here are the facts. Alexandria, Louisiana, June 2022, around 5 in the afternoon. Officers conducted a traffic stop that lasted about 21 minutes. Every citation was eventually dismissed. The driver had a firearm in the car and disclosed it. He was described as agitated and was wearing baggy clothing. Officers frisked him, then went into his pockets, then obtained what they called consent to search. He sued under the Fourth Amendment and also brought a First Amendment claim based on a right to record his own detention. The district court denied the officers qualified immunity, and they appealed. The holding. The denial of qualified immunity on the Fourth Amendment claims was affirmed on both prongs, per curiam, by a panel of Judges King, Smith, and Ramirez. There was no reasonable suspicion to justify the stop at its inception, and the lack of reasonable suspicion was clearly established. The frisk was unlawful, because agitation plus a lawfully possessed and disclosed firearm plus loose clothing does not establish that a person is armed and dangerous. The court cited its own decision in United States versus Wilson from last year for the proposition that an officer cannot search or seize a person simply because he is keeping or bearing a firearm. The pocket search exceeded Terry under Minnesota versus Dickerson, because continued exploration of the pockets went past the strictly circumscribed search for weapons, and because there was no break in the causal chain, any consent that followed was invalid. The officers won one piece: the panel reversed on the First Amendment right to record claim, on the clearly established prong only, holding that Turner versus Lieutenant Driver does not clearly establish a right to record one's own detention. What was not decided. Whether a First Amendment right to record one's own detention exists. The court expressly declined to say. What this means in practice. There was no force issue in this case at all, and the officers still lost on both prongs. The stop was bad, so the frisk was bad, so the pocket search was bad, so the consent was worthless. Everything downstream dies with the stop. In a growing number of states lawful carry is the norm, and the presence of a gun is no longer by itself a reason to touch anybody. How to train it. Frisk articulation has to be about behavior, not hardware. What did he do with his hands? What did he say? What did he reach for? Agitated is not a fact, it is a conclusion, and it will not survive a panel. Take baggy clothing out of the training vocabulary as a frisk justification; it appears in this opinion and the court rejected it flatly. If the agency's force review process starts at the moment hands go on, it starts too late. Ask why the person was stopped first, every single time.

Now the application, the part to take into a briefing room or a training block this week.

First, Merits holdings are worth more than immunity wins. Handy and Calloway both say the force was constitutional. Cite those. Stop citing cases where the officer only won because the plaintiff could not find a precedent close enough on the facts.

Second, Accumulation is the whole game. In both winning cases no single fact justified the force; the sequence did. Train officers to narrate the sequence in order, and to name the specific fact that moved them.

Third, Complete, clear video is case ending. Video incorporated into the complaint decided Handy at the pleading stage, before discovery. Review camera policy for what gets preserved, the angle, and the audio, so the recording captures the approach, the commands, and the subject's response.

Fourth, Document device failure in the sentence where it happens. The Taser that connected and did no good is what made Calloway a merits win. That fact belongs in the report where it happened, not in a supplemental.

Fifth, Force review starts at the reason for the contact. Rosales is the whole argument in six pages. The stop was bad, so the frisk, the pocket search, and the consent all fell with it. Ask why the person was stopped first, every time.

Sixth, Dispatch specificity is the officer's legal foundation. Assaulted his girlfriend, may have fired a round, outside waiting with a long gun: each fact a reasonable officer relied on in Handy came from the call taker. Train dispatchers that vague is expensive.
