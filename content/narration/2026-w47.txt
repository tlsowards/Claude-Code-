This is the Use of Force Case Law Update from Sowards Consulting, for the week of November 15 through 21, 2026. This update is written for trainers, supervisors, and the officers they train, in patrol, probation, custody, and juvenile settings. The order is always the same: federal law first, starting with the Ninth Circuit and the Supreme Court, then any other federal circuit or California decision that matters, and then application, meaning what to do with it on the street, in the jail, and in the juvenile hall. Every case ends with two things: what it means in practice, and how to train it.

Here is the short version. No published force opinion came out of the Ninth Circuit this week, but the court's one custody decision, Brown v. Adamson, shows how the Supreme Court's Zorn v. Linton standard now works in a prison medical case: two Nevada prison doctors who let a specialist referral wait for years got qualified immunity because no earlier case defined when a delayed referral becomes deliberate indifference. In the Sixth Circuit, a divided panel in Crowley v. Cassidy granted immunity to a Tennessee deputy who shot a man during a close quarters struggle after a sergeant yelled "Gun!" and two shots rang out; the dissent read the same record to show the man's hand on a headrest, nowhere near the gun in his pocket. Two unpublished Third Circuit decisions round out the week: Gregory v. Dawdy, where body camera video could not win officers an early dismissal of a punch and body slam claim, and Robertson v. United States, where a fugitive task force's compliance strikes on a murder suspect hiding under a bed with what looked like a rifle were found reasonable after a bench trial. The Supreme Court decided nothing on force and sent nothing back.

Now, the Ninth Circuit. One published decision this week, and it is a custody case about medical care rather than force. It belongs in a force brief because it is the circuit's clearest application so far of Zorn versus Linton to prison staff: the same clearly established standard that now governs an officer's wristlock governs a doctor's referral decision, and it protected the doctors here.

Brown versus Adamson. Ninth Circuit (from the District of Nevada, Judge Miranda M. Du), decided November 20, 2026, docket number 25-730. This is a published decision. Here are the facts. In June 2018 Jason Brown, an inmate at Nevada's Lovelock Correctional Center, was hit in the face with a softball while walking the track. A hospital CT scan showed a fractured left cheekbone, a plastic surgeon recommended surgery, Dr. Kim Adamson requested and received approval, and the surgery happened the next day. Afterward Brown kept seeing Dr. Adamson for complaints he says related to the head injury. In 2020 he filed multiple medical requests describing persistent pain, vertigo, and nausea; according to Brown, Dr. Adamson told him "there was nothing else that he could do" and to "continue taking the medication he prescribed." Dr. Adamson retired in March 2020 and Dr. Dana Marks replaced him in May. Around the same time all of the prison's medical specialists left, and decisions about outside care went to a Utilization Review Committee on which both doctors sat. Brown's symptoms continued: dizziness, weakness, vomiting, then vision that "had gone from blurred to double vision." Dr. Marks ordered labs, noted in June 2020 that Brown should see an ear, nose, and throat specialist, and ordered a spinal tap in August 2020, but did not discuss the results with Brown. In May 2021 Brown asked again to see the doctor for vertigo, loss of eyesight, tremors, numbness, headaches, and nausea; he was not seen until November and December 2021, when Dr. Marks again noted an ENT referral and added neurology. The record shows no referral request to the committee in June 2020 or December 2021. Brown sued pro se in December 2021. In January 2023 the district court granted a preliminary injunction in part, directing that Brown be seen by a neurologist and an ENT. Dr. Marks brought the referral to the committee around May 2023, after the injunction, and Brown saw both specialists in June 2023. The neurologist diagnosed chronic migraines and pulsatile headaches and prescribed a migraine drug; the ENT diagnosed vertigo, aura migraines, and pulsatile tinnitus. The district court denied the doctors summary judgment on qualified immunity, finding genuine disputes about deliberate indifference. The doctors appealed. The holding. Reversed and remanded with instructions to grant qualified immunity to both doctors. The panel went straight to the second prong: whether the law was clearly established with the specificity Zorn versus Linton requires. Under Zorn, "courts generally 'need to identify a case where an officer acting under similar circumstances . . . was held to have violated' the Constitution," the prior case "must define the right with a 'high degree of specificity,'" and "principles stated generally . . . do not suffice." The court said it was "mindful of the requisite high degrees of both (1) specificity in the established formulation of the law and (2) similarity between the facts of the prior and instant cases." Measured that way, none of Brown's three cases did the work. Hoptowit versus Ray, a 1982 prison conditions class action, said referrals must be "reasonably speedy" and that prison doctors must be able to treat problems or refer prisoners to others who can, but it "did not opine on the specific referral responsibilities of prison medical providers in the event multiple courses of treatment prove ineffective." Stewart versus Aranas involved staff who "never deviated from their 'wait and see' treatment plan" as a patient's condition "sharply deteriorated," while these doctors "developed a plan to further investigate his medical complaints but failed to follow through" and "tried several medical treatments and approaches." Snow versus McDaniel involved a committee that denied outside surgeons' recommendations three times, including after a doctor called the situation "potentially life-threatening," while here "the record does not indicate that the URC ever denied any referral request for Brown." The court concluded: "there is no clearly established law defining the point at which a delay in making an outside medical referral constitutes deliberate indifference under the Eighth Amendment," and "a reasonable prison medical provider surveying the case law here cannot ascertain the specific right to which Brown is entitled." The court restated the deliberate indifference framework along the way: a serious medical need, plus "a purposeful act or failure to respond to a prisoner's pain or possible medical need" and resulting harm; the indifference "must be substantial," not negligence or malpractice; and "mere delay" of care, "without more," is not enough. What was not decided. Whether either doctor was deliberately indifferent. The court decided only that the law was not clearly established, and it said the district court had found genuine factual disputes on the merits. Whether a delay of this length, standing alone, violates the Eighth Amendment. The court said no case had defined the point at which a delayed referral becomes deliberate indifference, which leaves the question open rather than answering it. Nothing about use of force. The lieutenant, coach, nurse, and other staff named in the complaint had been dismissed below and were not before the court. The preliminary injunction that produced the 2023 referrals was not at issue. What this means in practice. For custody medical staff and the supervisors who sit on review committees, Brown is both a shield and a warning. The shield: after Zorn, a prison provider is immune unless a prior case with closely similar facts held the same conduct unconstitutional, and the Ninth Circuit found none for a years long referral delay where the doctors kept examining, testing, and prescribing. The warning: the court reached that result only because the record showed effort. The doctors ordered labs, a spinal tap, and new medications, and the committee never refused a referral. The court contrasted staff who never changed a failing plan and a committee that denied surgery three times, and those cases still stand. What the record lacked, and what the court noticed, was any request to the committee in June 2020 or December 2021 even though the treating doctor had written the referral need in the chart and sat on the committee himself. A referral noted but never submitted is the fact pattern this opinion turns on. For custody officers, the practical point is the same one the deliberate indifference cases always carry: a prisoner's repeated written medical requests are the record. Brown's kites from 2020 and 2021 are what the court quoted. Officers who receive, log, and forward those requests, and who document when a prisoner was seen, are building the record that decides these cases either way. How to train it. Teach Zorn's two requirements as a pair, specificity and factual similarity, using Brown as the custody example alongside the force examples. Then teach the three cases the court distinguished, because they describe the conduct that still loses: a plan that never changes as a patient deteriorates, specialist recommendations ignored for years, and a committee that denies surgery after an outside doctor warns of a life threatening condition. For medical and classification supervisors, train the referral loop as a closed loop: a referral noted in a chart is submitted to the committee, the committee's decision is recorded, and the patient is told the result. Brown's case went to a federal appeals court because the note existed and the submission did not. For line officers, train medical request handling as evidence handling: date and time of receipt, where it went, and when the prisoner was seen. Teach the posture accurately. Brown granted immunity on the clearly established prong only; it did not say the care was adequate, and the district court had found disputes on that question.

Now, the Other federal circuits. Three unpublished decisions, none binding precedent, each worth a briefing. Crowley is a deadly force case decided on a shouted warning and two shots the deputy did not fire, with a dissent that reads the same half minute very differently. Gregory shows what body camera video can and cannot do for officers at the pleading stage. Robertson is a fugitive task force entry decided after a bench trial on state tort law, with a clear account of why compliance strikes to a resisting suspect's ribs were reasonable.

Crowley versus Cassidy. Sixth Circuit (from the Eastern District of Tennessee), decided November 16, 2026, docket number 26-5258. This decision is unpublished, so it is not binding precedent. Here are the facts. A Dollar General employee called 911 for a welfare check on Casey Crowley, who was slumped over the steering wheel of his running truck in the store's parking lot. Officers Beach and McCready arrived first. Roused, Crowley provided identification but "couldn't carry a coherent conversation." The officers repeatedly asked him to get out of the truck and he refused. Sergeant Nygaard arrived and asked again; Crowley refused again. The officers suspected drugs or alcohol. When Nygaard reached into the truck to take the keys from the ignition, Crowley grabbed his arm. McCready and Beach then grabbed Crowley to get him under control, and "the melee intensified." Nygaard shouted, "Gun! Gun! He's got a gun!" followed by, "I'll blow your effing head off!" Beach saw Nygaard draw. Seconds later Nygaard shot Crowley. A few seconds after that, Beach shot Crowley. The officers began CPR and called for help; Crowley died. Officers recovered a gun from Crowley's person and another from the truck, along with a bag of methamphetamine in his pocket; toxicology showed a high level of methamphetamine in his blood. The whole encounter "lasted about half a minute." The dissent adds the facts as the plaintiff's evidence showed them: when Crowley was shot, his left hand was holding Nygaard's arm and his right hand "was grabbing the truck's passenger-seat headrest"; the gun "remained in his right pocket"; Crowley "never fired, brandished, or attempted to brandish" it; Beach said he "saw a glimpse of something black in Crowley's hand" but could not say which hand; and Beach was close enough to be pulling on Crowley's sweatshirt hood when the shots were fired and to have blood on his face and uniform afterward. Crowley's wife sued both officers under section 1983 for excessive force. The district court denied qualified immunity to both. Only Beach appealed. The holding. Reversed as to Beach, in an opinion by Judge Thapar joined by Judge Batchelder, and remanded for the district court to address the state law claims. The court treated the imminent threat factor as decisive in a deadly force case: "the imminent-threat factor is the one that matters most," and, quoting Barnes versus Felix, the suspect's conduct during the encounter is "always relevant." On these facts, "Beach had ample reason to reasonably believe Casey posed an imminent and deadly threat": a close quarters brawl the man had started, a belief that he was intoxicated, a fellow officer's shout that he had a gun, then more yelling and two gunshots. "Only then did Beach fire." There was no "time or space" to "monitor Casey, issue a warning, or take cover." The court assumed Beach never saw a gun: "it doesn't matter whether Beach himself saw Casey brandishing a gun. Indeed, we assume for present purposes that he didn't. Even so, the key question is whether Nygaard's yell that Casey had a gun, immediately followed by two gunshots, could've made Beach believe Casey was somehow trying to use a gun. From the perspective of a reasonable officer in Beach's shoes, we must say yes." Active resistance, grabbing the sergeant's arm and disobeying repeated orders, also favored the deputy. On the second prong the court applied Zorn versus Linton: the plaintiff had to point to a case in which "an officer taking similar actions in similar circumstances was held to have violated" the Fourth Amendment, and "Crowley doesn't point to a single such case." The court relied on its own unpublished decision in Cooper versus City of Columbus, where an officer in a hand to hand struggle heard a fellow officer announce a gun, heard a gunshot, and fired, and the court found no violation even though the fellow officer had fired the shot and the suspect's gun stayed in his pocket. "When case law at the time of an officer's action suggests his action is lawful, its unlawfulness can't have been clearly established." On the dissent. Judge White dissented and would have affirmed the denial of immunity. Taking the evidence in the plaintiff's favor, "Crowley never fired, brandished, or attempted to brandish the gun in his right pocket," his right hand was on the headrest "and was nowhere near the gun," and "the only person Beach could have reasonably believed was 'trying to use a gun' was Nygaard," whom Beach had just heard threaten to shoot and seen draw. Relying on Floyd versus City of Detroit, where a second officer who heard a shot and saw his partner take cover shot an unarmed man with his hands up, she wrote that "had Beach looked at Crowley's hands even momentarily before shooting him, Beach would have seen that Crowley was not brandishing or attempting to brandish a gun." On the second prong she cited the circuit's rule that "individuals have a right not to be shot unless they are perceived as posing a threat to officers or others" and its holding that "merely possessing a weapon, without more, is insufficient to justify the use of deadly force against a suspect." What was not decided. Whether Sergeant Nygaard, who yelled and fired first, is entitled to immunity. He did not appeal, and the district court's denial as to him was not before the panel. The state law claims against Beach, which the district court had not addressed and which were remanded. Whether Crowley was in fact reaching for the gun; the majority assumed Beach did not see a gun and decided the case on what Beach heard. The decision is unpublished and binds no one. What this means in practice. Crowley is a second officer case, and that is how it should be read. The deputy who fired did not see a gun, did not see the man's hands clearly, and fired a few seconds after hearing a partner yell "Gun!" and two shots. The majority held that sequence, inside a half minute struggle the man had started, gave a reasonable officer cause to believe a deadly threat was present. The dissent held that a glance at the man's hands would have shown there was none, and that the circuit's cases already said possession of a gun is not enough. Three points for the street. First, what an officer hears from a partner is part of the totality, and the majority treated the shout plus the shots as the decisive facts. The dissent did not disagree that Beach heard them; it faulted him for not looking. The practical rule that satisfies both opinions is to see the hands before firing whenever the position allows it, and to be able to say afterward what was seen. Second, the words shouted in a struggle become the record. "Gun! Gun! He's got a gun!" was the fact that justified the second shooter. "I'll blow your effing head off!" was the fact the dissent used to argue that the only person trying to use a gun was the sergeant. Third, the crime severity factor carried almost no weight. The call was a welfare check and no crime was suspected at the start; the majority said the suspect's own conduct during the encounter is what matters most once a struggle begins. On the immunity side, the opinion is a reminder that after Zorn the plaintiff must find a case with these facts, and that a circuit's own unpublished decision finding similar conduct lawful can defeat the clearly established argument. How to train it. Run Crowley as a two officer scenario and debrief it from both opinions. Put the trainee in Beach's position: a partner reaches into a vehicle, the driver grabs the arm, the trainee is pulling on the driver's clothing, a shout of "Gun!" comes from the other side of the body, then two shots. Ask what the trainee can see, what the trainee knows, and what decision the trainee makes, and require the articulation to separate the two: what was seen and what was heard. Then show the dissent's facts, the hand on the headrest and the gun still in the pocket, and ask what a momentary look at the hands would have changed. Train the warning language. A shout that identifies the threat, "Gun, right pocket," gives a partner something to look for; a threat to shoot gives a plaintiff's lawyer something to quote. Train welfare checks on intoxicated drivers as the setting this case came from: a slumped driver, a running truck, repeated refusals, and a decision to reach into the vehicle for the keys. Discuss what alternatives existed before the reach and what each officer's position would be once the struggle began. Teach the posture: unpublished, decided on the plaintiff's version of the facts, with the first shooter's case still in the district court.

Gregory versus Dawdy. Third Circuit (from the Western District of Pennsylvania, Judge Susan Paradise Baxter), decided November 20, 2026, docket number 26-1486. This decision is unpublished, so it is not binding precedent. Here are the facts. Joseph Gregory sued four officers pro se under section 1983 over his arrest. His five page complaint alleged that one officer "struck the plaintiff in the face with a closed fist which resulted in physical damage to the plaintiff's mouth knocking his teeth out," and that the others "picked the plaintiff up off of his feet and body slammed the plaintiff to the ground after the plaintiff was already pinned against a parked car." The complaint referred to body camera footage twice: it alleged that the officer who struck him can be heard on body camera telling him, "You are right I knocked your tooth out," and that another officer, not a party, can be heard saying the officers were going to take him to the station and charge him instead of taking him to the hospital. The officers moved to dismiss on qualified immunity and submitted their body camera video, arguing the court should consider it because the complaint relied on it, or should convert the motion to one for summary judgment. The magistrate judge declined to convert the motion, and the district court adopted a recommendation denying immunity at the pleading stage because deciding whether the force was reasonable "requires a more developed factual record." The officers appealed. The holding. Affirmed, in an opinion by Judge Krause, without prejudice to the officers raising qualified immunity again at summary judgment. The officers did not dispute that, on the face of the complaint, they were not entitled to immunity. Their argument was that the video should have been considered. The court held the video was not "integral to or explicitly relied upon" in the complaint: the complaint alleged facts that "not even Appellants dispute are sufficient to state a claim for excessive force," its references to the footage were "few and fleeting," and the second reference concerned the medical claim rather than the force claim. The court lacked jurisdiction over the magistrate judge's refusal to convert the motion, which the officers had not appealed. Quoting its recent decision in Stringer versus County of Bucks, the court said "Rule 12(b)(6) is often 'a mismatch for qualified immunity and almost always a bad ground for dismissal,'" because the fact bound clearly established inquiry and "the dearth of facts at the pleading stage" can keep a court from seeing "the precise contours of the official's conduct." It remanded for discovery with a caution: qualified immunity exists to shield officials from "broad-ranging discovery," so the officers may re-raise immunity "in a motion for summary judgment at the earliest opportunity, and discovery in the first instance should be narrowly tailored to that defense." What was not decided. What the body camera video shows. The court never looked at it for the merits. Whether the punch or the body slam was excessive force, and whether the officers will be immune once the record is developed. Whether the refusal to convert the motion was correct; the court held that order was not before it. The decision is nonprecedential. What this means in practice. Gregory answers a question officers ask often: if the body camera shows what happened, why does the case go on? At the pleading stage the court reads only the complaint, and a complaint that alleges a closed fist to the face, teeth knocked out, and a body slam of a man already pinned against a car states a claim whatever the video shows. The video becomes the officers' evidence at summary judgment, which the court told the officers to seek at the earliest opportunity, with discovery limited to the immunity question. Two other points are worth a briefing. First, an officer's own recorded words are pleaded against him. The complaint's strongest line was a quotation attributed to the officer on his own camera, "You are right I knocked your tooth out." Second, the decision not to take an injured arrestee to the hospital, allegedly announced on camera, became a separate deliberate indifference claim that is still in the case. How to train it. Use Gregory to teach the stages of a force lawsuit, because officers who expect the video to end the case on day one lose confidence in a process that is working as designed. Walk through complaint, motion to dismiss, discovery, summary judgment, and trial, and show where the video enters. Teach post force conduct on camera: statements made to an arrestee after force are evidence of the force, and a comment that reads as an admission will be quoted in the complaint. Teach the medical decision as a separate duty: an arrestee with a mouth injury and missing teeth gets medical attention, and the decision and its reasons are recorded. Teach report writing to the pleading standard: the report should describe the resistance that preceded each application of force, including why force continued after the subject was against the car, because that is the allegation a court will test the video against.

Robertson versus United States. Third Circuit (from the Eastern District of Pennsylvania, Judge Harvey Bartle III), decided November 20, 2026, docket number 25-1401. This decision is unpublished, so it is not binding precedent. Here are the facts. On March 18, 2020, Jabree Robertson was wanted by the Chester, Pennsylvania police for first degree murder with a weapon. A task force of local and federal officers, including members of the United States Marshals Violent Crime Fugitive Task Force, went to the house where he was staying to arrest him. An adult let them in; Robertson's girlfriend confirmed he was upstairs but would not say whether he was armed. Robertson, 6 feet 4 inches and 200 pounds with a violent criminal history, ignored repeated calls to surrender and hid under a bed. Officers tried a remote camera without success. After twenty minutes with no response, officers went upstairs, cleared other rooms, moved an occupant out of another bedroom, and entered the bedroom. They cleared the closets, and an officer lifted the bed. Robertson was lying on his stomach with his hands underneath him. Another officer saw what he believed was an AR-15 rifle under Robertson and announced, "Gun!" It was later determined to be a paintball gun. One officer sat on Robertson and held his head down while others tried to pull his hands out from under him. Because he resisted releasing his hands, two officers delivered compliance strikes to his side, after which he was handcuffed. He was taken downstairs and turned over to local police. Later that day, at his lawyer's request, he was taken to a hospital, where he was found to have fractured ribs, abrasions to his face, and a left wrist injury that the records described as likely chronic. Robertson sued the United States under the Federal Tort Claims Act for assault and battery. After a bench trial on liability, the district court found the officers' force reasonable and entered judgment for the government. The holding. Affirmed, per curiam. Under Pennsylvania law an officer is not liable for assault and battery if he uses reasonable force to make a lawful arrest, and the district court properly applied the Graham factors together with the Third Circuit's additional factors: the physical injury, the possibility the person is violent or dangerous, the duration of the action, whether it occurred in an arrest, the possibility the suspect is armed, and the number of persons the officers must contend with. The court found no clear error. Robertson's testimony that he lost consciousness upstairs and "woke up at the bottom of the stairs" was discounted; five officers testified he walked down on his own. That the weapon was not in his hands and that he was never charged with resisting did not undermine the threat finding: he was wanted for murder with a firearm that had not been recovered, had a prior resisting conviction, had not surrendered through twenty minutes of calls, and his girlfriend would not say whether he was armed, so "the circumstances led to the reasonable conclusion that Robertson had the capacity for lethal violence, that he would not comply if confronted, and that he was probably armed." The absence of DNA testing or photographs of the paintball gun did not undermine the officers' testimony that it looked like a rifle because only the barrel and fore grip were visible. The officers made "split-second judgments" to prevent use of the weapon. "Within 45 seconds after the bed was lifted, Robertson was in handcuffs and the use of force ceased." "The fractured ribs are consistent with the use of compliance strikes in response to Robertson's resistance," and there was no credible evidence of a torn ligament or concussion. What was not decided. No Fourth Amendment claim was decided; the suit was a state law tort claim against the United States, although the court applied the same reasonableness factors. No individual officer was a defendant. The court reviewed findings of fact for clear error after a bench trial, so it did not decide what a jury could find on a different record. The decision is nonprecedential. What this means in practice. Robertson is a model of a force record that holds up. Nearly every fact the court relied on was created or preserved by the officers before the first strike: the warrant for murder with an unrecovered firearm, the twenty minutes of recorded calls to surrender, the attempt to locate him with a camera, the clearing of the other rooms and removal of an occupant, the girlfriend's refusal to say whether he was armed, and the announcement of "Gun!" when the bed came up. The force itself was described with a clock: hands under the body, a refusal to release them, compliance strikes to the ribs, handcuffs, and force stopped within 45 seconds. The injuries matched the force described, and the hospital record itself called the wrist injury likely chronic. Two cautions. The object was a paintball gun, and the court accepted the officers' belief because they explained what they could see, only the barrel and fore grip, under a prone man with his hands beneath him. An officer who announces "Gun!" should be able to describe what was visible. And the time between arrest and medical care was raised by the plaintiff; the injuries were documented only because his lawyer asked for a hospital visit the same day. Agencies should not rely on defense counsel to get an injured arrestee examined. How to train it. Use Robertson to teach the articulation of a warrant service on a violent fugitive: the known facts about the person, the surrender announcements and how long they lasted, the attempts to locate without entry, the sequence of the entry, and the moment the decision to use force was made. Train the "Gun!" announcement as a description, not only an alarm: what was seen, where, and why it looked like a firearm. Train compliance strikes with a stop point: the strikes were to the side, in response to hands held under the body, and ended when the hands came out. Have trainees narrate that sequence with times. Pair the case with Crowley from the same week: both turned on what an officer heard a partner announce, and both show why the announcement needs to be specific and the announcer needs to be able to explain it. Teach the medical step as part of the arrest: injuries from compliance strikes are evaluated promptly and recorded, so the record of the injury comes from the agency rather than from a lawyer's request hours later.

Now the application, the part to take into a briefing room or a training block this week.

First, Zorn now governs custody medical decisions too. The Ninth Circuit applied Zorn versus Linton's two requirements, a specific rule and closely similar facts, to prison doctors and granted immunity for a years long referral delay. The cases that still lose describe a plan that never changes as a patient deteriorates, specialist recommendations ignored for years, and a committee that denies surgery after a life threatening warning. Supervisors on review committees should know those three fact patterns by heart.

Second, A referral noted is not a referral made. Brown's chart said ENT in June 2020 and ENT and neurology in December 2021, and the record showed no request to the review committee on either date even though the treating doctor sat on it. Close the loop: the note, the submission, the decision, and the patient told. Line officers close their part of the loop by logging and forwarding every written medical request with the date received.

Third, See the hands before the second shot. In Crowley the deputy fired on a partner's shout of "Gun!" and two shots he did not fire. The majority found that reasonable inside a half minute struggle; the dissent said a momentary look at the hands would have shown a hand on a headrest and a gun still in a pocket. The rule that satisfies both opinions: when position allows, see the hands, and be able to say what was seen.

Fourth, Make the shout a description. "Gun! Gun! He's got a gun!" justified the second shooter in Crowley; "I'll blow your effing head off!" gave the dissent its argument that the only person trying to use a gun was the sergeant. In Robertson, "Gun!" was accepted because the officer could explain what he saw, a barrel and fore grip under a prone man. Announce what is seen and where. Leave threats out.

Fifth, The video does not end the case at the pleading stage. In Gregory the court read only the complaint, which alleged a closed fist to the face and a body slam of a man already pinned. The officers' body camera video enters at summary judgment, which the court told them to seek at the earliest opportunity. An officer's own recorded words, "You are right I knocked your tooth out," became the complaint's best line.

Sixth, Put a clock on the force and a stop point on the strikes. Robertson's record showed twenty minutes of surrender calls, a camera attempt, the entry sequence, hands under the body, compliance strikes to the side, and handcuffs within 45 seconds, after which force stopped. The injuries matched that account. Documentation of that quality, made at the time, is what won a bench trial.
