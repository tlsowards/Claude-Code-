This is the Use of Force Case Law Update from Sowards Consulting, for the week of October 12 through 18, 2026. This update is written for trainers, supervisors, and the officers they train, in patrol, probation, custody, and juvenile settings. The order is always the same: federal law first, starting with the Ninth Circuit and the Supreme Court, then the other federal circuits, then California, and then application, meaning what to do with it on the street, in the jail, and in the juvenile hall. Every case ends with two things: what it means in practice, and how to train it.

Here is the short version. A strong week for training material. The Tenth Circuit denied qualified immunity for a civil standby shooting of a man holding a bat at his side, and the Supreme Court left standing a second Tenth Circuit denial involving repeated Taser cycles and a fatal shot. The Eighth Circuit affirmed a jury verdict for two children locked in courthouse holding cells with no legal basis. The Sixth Circuit let a prison mold case go forward against nine officials, and the Seventh gave immunity to a deputy in a fatal distracted-driving crash.

Now, the Ninth Circuit. No use of force decision this week. Two items deserve a mention. Solorio v. Sullivan, an unpublished memorandum, affirmed dismissal of a California state prisoner's Eighth Amendment claims against a lieutenant and the warden because he never alleged facts showing they personally participated; the memorandum does not say what claim went to the jury, so whether it was a force case is unknown. The supervisor lesson is simple: supervisory liability still requires facts about what the supervisor personally did or failed to do. United States v. State of California, published, held the United States is likely to win its claim that California cannot directly regulate federal officers through section 10 of Senate Bill 805, the No Vigilantes Act, which requires non-uniformed federal officers to visibly display identification, and ordered a preliminary injunction against enforcing that section against federal officers. That is a preliminary ruling, not a final judgment, and the companion No Secret Police Act portion of the opinion was not analyzed for this brief.

Pitts versus Burke (leaving in place Burke versus Pitts, Tenth Circuit). Supreme Court of the United States; decision below from the Tenth Circuit, decided Review denied October 13, 2026, docket number 25-1062. The Supreme Court declined to review this case, which is not a ruling on the merits. Here are the facts. In June 2019, two Bartlesville, Oklahoma officers responded to a domestic disturbance call from a father who said his son, Thomas Gay, was acting erratically and might be on drugs. The father said Thomas was unarmed but warned that he kept making movements toward his back pocket. Inside, Thomas was sweating and holding what appears to have been a ventriloquist doll. One officer told him once to drop it, pointed a Taser, and with no further commands tased him. Thomas backed into a bedroom, started to lie down, stood back up, and was tased again. The Taser did not incapacitate him, which the district court found was due to user error. After a brief struggle, Thomas moved toward the bedroom door and moved his hand toward his back pocket, and the second officer shot him twice. He was dead within about three minutes of the officers' arrival. Whether he was holding anything when he was shot is disputed. The National Sheriffs' Association and the National Fraternal Order of Police filed briefs supporting the officers, and the Court asked for a response in April. The holding. The Tenth Circuit affirmed the denial of qualified immunity for both officers. On the Taser, it was clearly established that repeatedly tasing, without warning, a person who is not resisting and has not been told he is being detained violates the Fourth Amendment. On the shooting, relying on Tennessee versus Garner and circuit precedent, it was clearly established that an officer may not shoot a moving but unarmed and unthreatening person. The court rejected the argument that the autopsy and the officers' testimony blatantly contradicted the district court's findings, and approved consideration of whether the officers recklessly created the need for force. The Supreme Court denied review with no noted dissent. What was not decided. A cert denial is not a ruling on the merits. The case goes back to the trial court. What this means in practice. One command followed by repeated Taser cycles without warning, on a person who is not resisting and has not been told he is being detained, was held clearly unlawful. And a disputed, unarmed hand movement did not justify deadly force at the summary judgment stage. How to train it. Warn when you can, and give people time to comply. Tell the person he is being detained before force is used. Teach officers to articulate the specific hostile motion they saw, not just that the person moved. Train supervisors to look for that motion on the video before signing off on a report.

Frasure versus City of Wyoming, Ohio (leaving in place the Sixth Circuit's grant of immunity). Supreme Court of the United States; decision below from the Sixth Circuit, May 2026, decided Review denied October 13, 2026, docket number 26-5411. The Supreme Court declined to review this case, which is not a ruling on the merits. Here are the facts. Shortly after midnight in January 2023, a neighbor reported flashlights and silhouettes in a building he thought was vacant. In fact, the family of a woman who had recently died was cleaning out her apartment. Three officers responded. Behind the building, one officer found the father next to a van and his son Joe in the driver's seat and ordered Joe out. The van backed into an obstruction, then accelerated forward, and the officer had to jump out of the way. As the van drove toward a slope where a second officer was running, that officer and a third fired. Joe was struck in the head and died. About five seconds passed between the van accelerating and the shots. The holding. The Sixth Circuit, in an unpublished opinion, held the plaintiffs had not identified clearly established law specific enough to put these officers on notice, distinguishing its earlier vehicle cases where the officer was never in the path of the car. The district court had excluded the plaintiffs' police practices expert for failing to meet federal disclosure rules; the Sixth Circuit said any error was harmless and noted that the report offered better approaches that were apparent with hindsight. The Supreme Court denied review. What was not decided. Nothing on the merits from the Supreme Court. Unpublished below. What this means in practice. An officer in the path of a moving vehicle is in a very different legal position from one who is not. And an expert report built on hindsight, or filed late, may never be heard. How to train it. For expert witnesses and reviewers: hindsight is not the standard. Anchor every opinion in the video and in accepted practice, and meet every disclosure deadline to the letter. For officers: position so that you are never the thing a vehicle has to drive through.

Now, the Other federal circuits.

Vision Bank versus Avery. Tenth Circuit, decided October 13, 2026, docket number 25-6140. This is a published decision. Here are the facts. In July 2020, an officer was assigned a civil standby so a woman could pick up her belongings from a home where Christopher Poor was drinking. She described him as mean. Christopher walked in yelling, "where's the police," holding an aluminum baseball bat down at his side. The officer drew his gun and ordered him to drop it. Christopher said he would, now that the officer was there. He walked away, turned back, said he was putting it down, switched the bat between his hands, and pointed a finger at the officer. As the officer gave his third command to set it down, Christopher took one step in the officer's general direction while moving the bat back to his right hand. The officer fired three shots and killed him. Sixteen seconds passed from the moment Christopher walked in to the moment he was shot. The holding. Affirmed the denial of qualified immunity. None of the Graham factors supported deadly force: there was no crime at issue on a civil standby, Christopher was not resisting or fleeing, and a jury could find he made no hostile motion with the bat. The officer argued the video blatantly contradicted that finding; the court said the video shows at most a single step, and the moment the officer pointed to was the same second he was firing. Three earlier Tenth Circuit cases clearly established that an officer may not use deadly force against a person holding something other than a gun who is not charging and makes no slicing, stabbing, or hostile motion. No dissent. The city had already won summary judgment, so the case proceeds against the officer alone. What this means in practice. A weapon held down is not a weapon raised. A person who says he is going to comply deserves a moment to do it. How to train it. Treat civil standbys as potential domestic violence calls: get the history, plan positions, bring a second officer. Against an impact weapon that is not being raised, distance and a barrier are the answer. Train officers to articulate the specific hostile motion they saw, not just that he had a bat.

Rockett versus Eighmy. Eighth Circuit, decided October 13, 2026, docket number 25-1664. This is a published decision. Here are the facts. After a custody hearing in Missouri, two children refused to leave the courthouse with their mother. The judge took them to a conference room and threatened them with foster care. Then he took them to the juvenile division, led them to two holding cells, and had a juvenile officer lock them up. The officer patted them down and had them remove their shoes and socks. After 20 to 40 minutes they agreed to go with their mother. A jury awarded each child $5,000, and the court awarded more than $326,000 in attorney fees. The holding. Affirmed. Ordering the children locked in cells was a seizure, even though someone else closed the doors. It was unreasonable because there was no probable cause, no court order, and no emergency. Refusing to leave with a parent is not a jailable offense. The law was clearly established and this was an obvious case. The judge's good faith belief that he was helping did not change the answer. No dissent. What was not decided. The juvenile officer's own liability was not before the court. What this means in practice. No youth goes into a holding cell or a room without a documented legal basis: a detention order, an arrest on probable cause, or a defined emergency. How to train it. Train staff to stop and call a supervisor when an instruction to confine a young person has no paperwork behind it. Do not assume an instruction from a judge or a supervisor protects the person who turns the key. Build the legal-basis check into the room confinement and court holding procedure.

Martinez versus Hidalgo County. Fifth Circuit, decided October 15, 2026, docket number 25-40464. This decision is unpublished, so it is not binding precedent. Here are the facts. In 2021, deputies in Weslaco, Texas responded to a domestic call. A mother said her son had punched her, had bipolar disorder, was off his medication, and had locked himself inside. A window shattered from inside. A woman outside shouted at the deputies in Spanish to break down the door, and they forced entry. Inside, the son held an eight-inch kitchen knife, pointed it at them, shut his bedroom door, and ignored commands. About a minute later he opened the door and walked toward the deputies with the knife in a small, cluttered space. They fired 11 times and stopped when he fell. He died that day. The holding. Affirmed summary judgment for the deputies. Valid consent to enter from the mother, probable cause to arrest, and deadly force was reasonable when he approached with the knife at close range. The deputies were not required to try less lethal options first. No dissent. The court called the possibility that the deputies did not follow the county's high-risk and barricaded subject policy concerning, though it did not change the constitutional result. What this means in practice. The constitutional floor is not the standard. A person in crisis, alone in his own home, with the person he hurt safely outside, is a candidate for containment, time, and a crisis team. How to train it. Forcing entry to make an arrest can shrink time and distance to nothing. In California, Penal Code section 835 a makes those earlier tactical decisions part of the analysis. Train containment and crisis response for a barricaded subject when nobody else is at risk, and train officers to follow the agency's own barricade policy, because a court will notice when they do not.

Bailey versus Michigan Department of Corrections. Sixth Circuit, decided October 15, 2026, docket number 25-1608. This is a published decision. Here are the facts. Women held at the Huron Valley prison in Michigan allege pervasive mold for years, including mold dripping from shower ceilings onto them, with breathing problems, rashes, infections, and in one case seizures. They allege they filed grievances and wrote letters, that officials had the mold painted over, and that the warden refused to provide fungus remover and said that if they scrubbed harder the mold would be gone. These are allegations at the pleading stage. The holding. Qualified immunity denied to nine officials, including the department director, the warden, deputy wardens, and the physical plant staff who allegedly painted over the problem. Immunity granted to five central office administrators because the complaint lumped them together with no specific facts. On the dissent. Judge Gilman would have kept the five central office administrators in the case. What this means in practice. An environmental grievance is a safety report, and a cover-up is worse than the hazard. How to train it. Route environmental grievances to someone with the authority to fix the problem, document the response, and never paint over a hazard. Supervisors who receive complaints and do nothing are the ones who stay in the case.

Bonner versus Ward. Eleventh Circuit, decided October 14, 2026, docket number 24-13016. This decision is unpublished, so it is not binding precedent. Here are the facts. A man in a Georgia prison was assaulted, then reported threats against his life. Staff moved him twice, ending in an administrative segregation unit. Twenty days later his cellmate killed him overnight. The holding. Affirmed dismissal. Moving him away from known threats was a reasonable response, and the complaint did not allege that anyone knew this particular cellmate was a danger to him. Qualified immunity was not reached. What this means in practice. A protective move is only as good as the screening of the new cellmate. How to train it. Document the classification review before double-celling someone who is under threat, and do cell checks and room checks on time and on paper.

Estate of Stinson versus Streicher. Seventh Circuit, decided October 14, 2026 (amended the following week to correct the spelling of Ceasar Stinson's name and adjust one sentence; result unchanged), docket number 22-3043. This is a published decision. Here are the facts. A Milwaukee County deputy on routine patrol, driving at the speed limit, looked down at his phone or dashboard computer for about 15 and a half seconds, ran a red light, and killed another driver. The holding. Reversed the denial of qualified immunity. Under County of Sacramento versus Lewis, a crash violates due process only if it shocks the conscience. Distracted driving was negligence, perhaps gross negligence, but not deliberate indifference, and no case clearly established otherwise. State law claims were not before the court. What this means in practice. The constitutional claim failed, but a person is dead and state tort exposure remains. The lesson is about vehicle operations, not the Constitution. How to train it. No device use while the car is moving outside a real emergency. Pull over to run queries. Supervisors should audit phone and computer activity after every collision.

Fox versus City of Jackson. Sixth Circuit, decided October 13, 2026, docket number 25-1864. This decision is unpublished, so it is not binding precedent. Here are the facts. A city obtained an administrative warrant to inspect a rental home. A tenant refused to unlock the door or produce identification. The officer arrested her for obstruction, then opened the lock himself with a credit card. The charge was later dismissed. The holding. Qualified immunity granted on the arrest because no clearly established law said the command to unlock the door was unlawful. The claim that the handcuffs were too tight failed because the video and her own testimony showed she never complained about them. What this means in practice. An arrest that adds nothing to a lawful entry adds risk without benefit, and a handcuff claim lives or dies on whether the person complained and what the camera shows. How to train it. Ask whether an arrest adds anything when there is already a lawful way in. Document handcuff fit every time, and say on camera that the cuffs were checked and double locked.

Now, the California. No California Supreme Court or Court of Appeal use of force decision this week. One related unpublished case, Escamilla v. Iredale from the Fourth District, cannot be cited in California courts: after a fatal San Diego police shooting in 2015, the family's civil rights lawyers sued a bail fugitive recovery agent whose wanted poster and tips described the man as armed and dangerous. The agent won, then sued the lawyers for malicious prosecution, and the Court of Appeal ruled for the lawyers. The training point: the information an officer receives before a deadly force encounter, and where it came from, becomes evidence and will be tested.

Now the application, the part to take into a briefing room or a training block this week.

First, A weapon held down is not a weapon raised. Vision Bank and Burke both turned on whether there was a hostile motion. Train officers to articulate the specific motion they saw, and train supervisors to look for it on the video.

Second, Warnings and commands matter. One command followed by repeated Taser cycles without warning, on someone who was not resisting and had not been told he was being detained, was held clearly unlawful. Warn when you can, tell the person he is being detained, and give time to comply.

Third, The video is the record. In both Tenth Circuit cases the court rejected the officers' arguments that the evidence blatantly contradicted the findings. Teach officers to describe what they perceived honestly and not to overclaim what the camera captured.

Fourth, Federal immunity varies by circuit; California law does not. The Fifth and Sixth Circuits protected close-range knife and vehicle shootings this week. California officers answer to Penal Code section 835a and their own policies. Train to that standard, not to the most forgiving court in the country.

Fifth, In custody, no legal basis means no cell. Rockett is a lesson for every juvenile hall and court holding area. Bailey and Bonner show where custodial liability usually lives: ignored hazards, housing decisions, and documentation.

Sixth, A cert denial is not a merits ruling. Pitts and Frasure leave the lower court decisions in place, but neither became Supreme Court law.
