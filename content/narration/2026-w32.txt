Smith versus Olvera. Ninth Circuit, decided July 14, 2026, docket number 25-1809. This is a published decision. A verification note before the facts. This brief was built from a machine transcript. The case name is given consistently as Smith v. Olvera and the plaintiff's name as James Driscoll Smith; check both spellings against the opinion. The majority quotation is reproduced as transcribed, including the phrase any reasonable officer, and should be verified against the slip opinion. The episode did not say whether the opinion was read in full. Smith's lawyers may still ask the full Ninth Circuit to rehear the case en banc, so the decision is not necessarily the last word; as of the episode it was binding panel authority in the Ninth Circuit. Here are the facts. The encounter happened at night in November 2022 in Elko, Nevada. James Driscoll Smith, a 62-year-old man, led Elko officers on a vehicle pursuit of roughly six minutes. During the pursuit he drove recklessly and ultimately crashed his truck into police vehicles and into a tree. When the truck finally stopped, Smith was taken to the ground and ended up lying face down. It was dark. Officer Brian Olvera then struck Smith multiple times with a baton, including strikes to the head. Smith suffered a broken arm and several broken teeth. The parties disagreed about one fact. Smith later said his hands were stretched out to his sides. Olvera said one of Smith's hands was near his waist. That dispute, hands out to the sides versus a hand drifting toward the waistband, is the whole case. Smith is now serving a prison sentence for the crimes he committed against officers during the pursuit. The qualified immunity question reached the Ninth Circuit from a summary judgment ruling. The holding. Qualified immunity granted, two to one, in an opinion joined by Judge Morgan Christen and Judge Danielle Forrest. The majority decided the case on the clearly established prong of the qualified immunity test, not on a clean finding that the force was reasonable. Given the darkness, the reckless conduct that preceded the stop, and Olvera's stated belief that Smith's hand was moving toward his waist, the court wrote, as the episode quoted it, that it could not conclude that any reasonable officer would have known that striking Smith in the head with a baton was unconstitutional. In plain terms, the majority did not say the head strikes were lawful. It said no prior case with close enough facts had put the officer on fair notice that they were unlawful. The court found no closely comparable precedent, so the officer keeps his immunity even if the underlying conduct might have crossed a constitutional line. That is the specificity approach to qualified immunity that courts have applied throughout the year. As it stands, the decision is binding panel authority in the Ninth Circuit, which includes Washington, Oregon, and California, and it is the most current appellate statement on baton force and immunity. On the dissent. Judge Sidney Thomas dissented, and his point was narrow and sharp. Even assuming Smith was armed, the Ninth Circuit has repeatedly held that officers may use deadly force, and baton strikes to the head can qualify as deadly force, only when a suspect can reasonably be perceived to be taking furtive or threatening actions. Whether Smith was actually taking such actions, or was already incapacitated and simply lying there after a violent crash, is a disputed question of fact, and disputed questions of fact belong to a jury, not to a judge deciding immunity on summary judgment. The dissent would have let the case go to trial. The majority did not dispute that head strikes with an impact weapon are deadly force. What was not decided. Whether the head strikes were constitutionally reasonable. The majority ruled only that the law was not clearly established. The margin was one judge: a different read of one disputed hand movement in the dark would have sent the case to a jury. A petition for rehearing en banc remained possible. What this means in practice. A baton strike to the head is not pain compliance. It is the impact weapon equivalent of a firearm and has to be justified by the same kind of immediate deadly threat. Most agency policies already classify intentional baton strikes to the head, neck, or throat as deadly force or as force likely to cause serious injury or death. An officer who defaults to head strikes because a subject is non-compliant rather than actively deadly is building a case the agency may not be able to defend on the merits, even if a court later hands over immunity on notice grounds. The officer's account of a hand moving toward the waist carried the day on immunity, but only by one vote. If the body camera clearly captured the hand movement, it is not a close case; if it did not, the officer is relying on his word against the plaintiff's word in a summary judgment fight. How to train it. Four points. First, lead with this: head strikes with an impact weapon are deadly force. Teach target zones that keep non-compliance responses off the head, neck, and throat unless a genuine deadly threat justifies lethal force. Second, do not teach Smith versus Olvera as a green light. The court did not bless the head strikes; it ruled on notice. The next officer, on slightly different facts or in a jurisdiction with a closer precedent, will not get the same benefit. Train to reasonableness under the totality of the circumstances, not to the immunity floor. Third, in low light on a prone subject, the perceived threat has to be narrated out loud in real time and, wherever possible, captured on video. The words hands, waistband, and stop reaching, spoken clearly while the camera rolls, are worth more than a page of after-action report written the next morning. Fourth, reassess after the crash. A subject who just hit a tree and police vehicles may be dazed, injured, or incapacitated rather than resisting. Train officers and first-line supervisors that the tactical picture changes the instant a fleeing subject is stopped and down. The question is no longer catch him; it is whether this person is still a threat right now, and that usually takes a beat of assessment before force continues. The dissent is, at bottom, an argument that the officer skipped that beat.

Now, the Supreme Court. One certiorari denial, below. No merits decision was discussed. The recurring anchors the episode identified have not moved: Graham v. Connor (1989) supplies the reasonableness standard, judged from the perspective of a reasonable officer on the scene; Tennessee v. Garner (1985) supplies the deadly force floor, no deadly force against a subject who does not pose an immediate threat of serious harm; Barnes v. Felix (May 2025) requires that reasonableness be judged on the totality of the circumstances, including what happened before the final moment, not on a frozen snapshot of the last second; and the specificity line from Zorn v. Linton and Smith v. Scott governs qualified immunity, meaning an officer keeps immunity unless a prior case defined the unlawfulness of the specific conduct with real particularity.

The McBride case (City of Los Angeles petition for certiorari). Supreme Court of the United States, reviewing the en banc Ninth Circuit, decided June 22, 2026 (certiorari denied). The Supreme Court declined to review this case, which is not a ruling on the merits. A verification note before the facts. The episode gave no full case caption and no Supreme Court docket number; it referred to the matter as the McBride case and to the City of Los Angeles as the petitioner. It did not give the date of the Ninth Circuit's en banc decision. A certiorari denial is not a ruling on the merits and sets no nationwide precedent. Check the caption, the docket, and the vote count against the court records. Here are the facts. In April 2020, Daniel Hernandez, who was alleged to be under the influence of methamphetamine, crashed his truck into several vehicles near downtown Los Angeles. He got out holding a knife and walked toward Los Angeles Police Department Officer Tony McBride. She repeatedly ordered him to drop the knife. He did not. She fired. The federal courts agreed that McBride had reason to fire her first four shots. The dispute is about the final two shots, fired after Hernandez had fallen to the ground. The Ninth Circuit, sitting en banc, ruled by a six to five vote that a jury could find those last two shots crossed the line, because in the roughly one-second pause between the fourth shot and the fifth, McBride, in the court's words, could have and should have reassessed the situation and possibly concluded that the threat had ended. The City of Los Angeles asked the Supreme Court to step in and shut the case down before trial. McBride is a former officer. The holding. On June 22, 2026, the Supreme Court declined to hear the city's appeal. The refusal is not a ruling on the merits, and it does not set nationwide precedent the way a signed opinion would. What it does is leave the en banc Ninth Circuit decision in place and send the question of the last two shots to a Los Angeles jury, so the civil excessive force trial against the former officer may proceed. The episode paired the denial with the reassessment theme in the Olvera dissent: every application of deadly force must be independently justified at the moment it is used, and a threat that justified shots one through four does not automatically justify shots five and six. When a subject goes down, when the knife is no longer advancing, and when the gap between rounds gives an officer even a sliver of time to perceive a change, the law increasingly expects the officer to perceive it. What this means in practice. In the litigation that follows a fatal shooting, the fight will very often be about the final rounds, the ones fired after the subject started to fall. That is the fact pattern that survives summary judgment and reaches a jury. The Supreme Court's refusal to rescue the city shows that the immunity shield does not always hold. How to train it. Train officers to shoot to stop the threat and then to assess, not to empty a magazine on a plan formed a second and a half earlier. Shoot or strike to stop the threat, then read the threat again before the next application. Train command staff that the final rounds are where the case will be fought, and that under the Barnes totality framework the transition point, where a threat may have ended and force continued, is where courts are looking.

Now the application, the part to take into a briefing room or a training block this week.

First, Baton strikes to the head are deadly force, full stop. Smith v. Olvera does not change that, and the officer's immunity does not make head strikes a sanctioned tactic. Teach impact weapons to the deadly force standard and keep non-compliance responses off the head, neck, and throat unless a genuine deadly threat justifies lethal force.

Second, An immunity win is not a green light. The Ninth Circuit ruled on notice, not on reasonableness. The next officer, on slightly different facts or where a closer precedent exists, will not get the same benefit. Train to the constitutional standard, reasonableness under the totality of the circumstances, not to the immunity floor.

Third, Narrate the threat in real time, on camera. The Olvera case turned on one disputed hand movement in the dark and came within one judge of a jury trial. In low light on a prone subject, say hands, waistband, and stop reaching out loud while the camera rolls. Clean video and clean narration are what tip a close read in the officer's favor.

Fourth, Reassess the instant a fleeing subject is stopped and down. A person who just crashed into a tree may be dazed or incapacitated rather than arming himself. The question is no longer catch him; it is whether he is still a threat right now. Cover the hands, control the arms, and create the half second of assessment before force continues.

Fifth, Every round must be justified on its own. In McBride, shots one through four were justified and shots five and six, fired after the man fell, are going to a jury. Shoot to stop the threat, then assess. Command staff should expect the litigation after a fatal shooting to focus on the final rounds.

Sixth, Run the Monday hypothetical. Two officers pursue a reckless driver who clips a patrol car and crashes. He is pulled out, prone, unresponsive, in poor light, and one officer with a baton in hand thinks he sees a right hand move toward the waistband. Walk the squad through the impact weapon question, the reassessment question, and the documentation question.
