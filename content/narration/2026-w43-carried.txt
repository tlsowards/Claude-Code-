Now, the Ninth Circuit. One carried case, decided September 15, 2026, not during the week covered. Read it next to Ruffin v. Davis in the Other federal circuits section: same framework, Graham, the totality of the circumstances, the immediate threat factor, and opposite results. The difference is what each man was doing with the gun and toward the officers. A worked hypothetical for the classroom: two officers respond to a report that a man is outside a house waving a shotgun after neighbors heard what sounded like shots. He is at the end of the driveway, refuses orders to drop the weapon and raise his hands, curses, swings the shotgun up over his head with one hand, and steps toward the officers. Then change one fact. A second man, also carrying a handgun, runs from the same house away from the officers, ignores commands, crouches by a fence, and turns his head toward them, with the gun in his hand but hanging at his side. Ask the class three questions. For each man, what exactly did the weapon do and where was he moving? Which looks more like Handy and which more like Ruffin, and what in the officer's articulation would make the difference? For a launcher situation, what steps stand between the officer and an unintended round? Then give the class a crowd control deployment and ask what the load verification step is and who witnesses it.

Handy versus Jones. Ninth Circuit (from the District of Alaska), decided September 15, 2026, docket number 25-5105. This is a published decision. A verification note before the facts. Carried forward for training; this opinion was filed September 15, 2026, not during the week this brief covers. The summary was built from the opinion as retrieved and summarized, not from a line-by-line read, so verify any quotation against the slip opinion before using it in a report or in testimony. Judge Callahan wrote the opinion; the names of the two judges who joined her were not clear in the source and are omitted here. This case is also covered in the September 26 brief. Here are the facts. Around midnight on May 13, 2024, a neighbor in Anchorage called 911 more than once. She reported screaming, a thud against a wall, and what sounded like two shots from Christopher Handy's apartment, and she said Handy was standing at the end of the driveway holding a shotgun, waiting for police. Four Anchorage officers arrived, announced themselves, and ordered Handy out. He came out with the shotgun. He refused repeated orders to raise his hands and drop it, waved the weapon overhead, and cursed at the officers. As he stepped off the curb toward the parking area, the four officers fired and shot him nine times. He died. His family sued for excessive force under the Fourth Amendment and for loss of familial association. The officers moved to dismiss on qualified immunity, and the district court denied the motion. The holding. Reversed in two steps, in an opinion by Judge Callahan. First, applying the Graham versus Connor factors and the totality of the circumstances approach the Supreme Court required in Barnes versus Felix, the panel held there was no constitutional violation. Officers had reason to suspect a violent crime, possibly shots fired inside a home. Handy was resisting by refusing to drop a shotgun. Most important, he posed an immediate threat by waving that shotgun while advancing toward the officers. The panel said the district court used the wrong standard when it asked whether a reasonable fact finder could conclude there was no immediate threat, and it reasoned that officers do not have to wait for a suspect to aim a gun at them before they act. Second, even if there had been a violation, the law was not clearly established. The plaintiffs cited cases in which the Ninth Circuit had denied immunity, and the panel distinguished each on its facts; no cited case required officers to wait until a weapon was aimed. The court vacated the denial of immunity and sent the case back with instructions to grant qualified immunity to all four officers. It also held it had jurisdiction over the interlocutory appeal even though the officers had moved to dismiss rather than for summary judgment. What this means in practice. An armed, belligerent person can pose an immediate threat even when the gun is never pointed at an officer. What made the threat immediate in Handy was the combination: a visible long gun, a call history that included possible shots fired, repeated refusal to drop the weapon, waving it overhead, and forward movement toward four officers. Armed plus belligerent plus defiance of commands plus advancing can be enough, even with the muzzle never pointed at anyone. Handy binds only in the Ninth Circuit. Elsewhere it is persuasive, and agencies should check their own circuit's law. How to train it. Teach the vocabulary of the weapon. Not that he had a gun, but where the muzzle was pointed, what the hands were doing, which way the person was moving, and what he said or did in response to commands. Teach that defiance is not a threat by itself. In Handy, ignoring commands became meaningful only because it was paired with a visible long gun, a violent call history, and forward movement. The officer has to be able to say in specific physical terms what the person did with the weapon and toward the officers. The training point is not that armed means shoot, and it is not that armed means wait.

Reinink versus Hart. Supreme Court of the United States, decided May 26, 2026, docket number 25-179. The Supreme Court declined to review this case, which is not a ruling on the merits. A verification note before the facts. This denial was issued May 26, 2026, not during the week this brief covers; it is carried forward for training. Built from the materials as retrieved and summarized, not a line-by-line read. The officer's name was rendered as both Reinink and Rehnink in the source transcript; Reinink is the spelling used most often and is used here. Here are the facts. Officer Reinink petitioned for review of the Sixth Circuit's published decision in Hart versus City of Grand Rapids, which denied him qualified immunity for firing a Speed Heat tear gas canister from a 40mm launcher at close range at an unarmed protester, after Reinink said he believed he had loaded a Muzzle Blast crowd control round instead. The petition asked two questions: whether the officer's intended level of force matters in choosing between the deadly force standard and the general force standard, and whether an officer who used more force than he intended can still claim qualified immunity if his mistake was reasonable. The full facts and the Sixth Circuit's reasoning, including Judge Larson's partial dissent, are in the Other federal circuits section of this brief. The holding. Certiorari denied on May 26, 2026. A cert denial is not a merits ruling and is not precedent. Justices Thomas and Alito would have granted the petition and summarily reversed, essentially for the reasons in Judge Larson's separate opinion below. The Court did not answer either question presented, so for now the operative decision is the Sixth Circuit's published opinion, under which the force is judged by what the munition could do at that range, not by what the officer meant to fire. That rule governs in the Sixth Circuit; the denial itself adds nothing to it and settles nothing for other circuits. What this means in practice. The Supreme Court has not said whether an officer's intent affects which force standard applies, or whether a reasonable mistake about the munition supports immunity. Until it does, the Sixth Circuit answer stands: an honest mistake about the round does not change how a court analyzes the shot. How to train it. Teach officers that the intent question is unresolved at the Supreme Court and that the Sixth Circuit's answer is the one in force there. Do not teach a cert denial as if it were a ruling. Pull each opinion from the court's own website before citing it in a lesson plan.

Ruffin versus Davis. Fourth Circuit (from the District of South Carolina), decided April 29, 2026, docket number 25-1318. This is a published decision. A verification note before the facts. Carried forward for training; filed April 29, 2026, not during the week this brief covers. Built from the opinion as retrieved and summarized, not a line-by-line read, so verify quotations against the slip opinion. The episode also corrected its own earlier description from May 9 that the Fourth Circuit held an officer cannot shoot because a suspect glanced backward. That overstated the holding: the opinion does not announce a standalone rule about head turns, and the earlier phrasing should be disregarded. The May 9 brief carries this case as first reported. Here are the facts. In April 2020, during a curfew, Columbia, South Carolina officer Kevin Davis saw a high school senior, identified in the opinion by the initials JR, walking alone. JR ran. Davis chased him for roughly 42 seconds, shouting commands that JR ignored. JR crouched briefly near a parking lot fence; the parties dispute whether that was deliberate or a trip. He stood and kept running. Davis fired 10 shots, and one struck JR in the forehead and killed him. A gun was recovered near JR's hand, and forensic evidence showed it had not been fired. Davis told investigators that JR turned his head toward him in what Davis believed was an attempt to turn his body and raise the firearm. The court noted that the only undisputed movement in the final moments was JR turning to face Davis, and, viewing that in the plaintiff's favor, said it was not a movement that could reasonably be perceived as dangerous. The district court denied qualified immunity and Davis appealed. The holding. Affirmed, in an opinion by Chief Judge Diaz, joined by Judges Thacker and Berner. On the merits, the severity of the crime factor favored JR, the evading arrest factor favored Davis, and the immediate threat factor, the most important, favored JR. Being armed and ignoring commands was not enough without a furtive or threatening movement with the weapon. On clearly established law, the court held that by April 2020 it was clearly established that an officer could not use deadly force on a fleeing, armed suspect who made no furtive or threatening movement with the weapon. One procedural point matters. The Fourth Circuit could not review the district court's finding that facts were genuinely disputed; it could only ask whether the undisputed facts showed a violation of clearly established law. The opinion does not announce a standalone rule about head turns. It holds that on this record, the head turn was not a movement reasonably perceived as dangerous. What this means in practice. Armed alone is not enough under Ruffin. A fleeing person whose gun is never pointed, never raised, and never fired, and who makes no threatening movement with it, cannot be shot under Fourth Circuit law, and that was clearly established by April 2020. An officer's account of a head turn or a perceived intent can be set against what was undisputed and viewed in the plaintiff's favor. Ruffin binds only in the Fourth Circuit and is persuasive elsewhere; agencies in other circuits should check their own circuit law. How to train it. Teach the pause. Ruffin involved 10 shots at a person who was running, and the question after each shot is the same as it has always been: what is he doing right now? For command staff, review how reports and interviews capture the final second. Interview protocols should ask what the officer saw, not what the officer concluded the person intended. Review the policy on foot pursuits of armed suspects, including when to stop, contain, and call for resources, because a 42-second chase ended in a 10-shot shooting.

Hart versus City of Grand Rapids. Sixth Circuit, decided May 15, 2025. This is a published decision. A verification note before the facts. Carried forward for training; filed May 15, 2025, not during the week this brief covers. The reporter citation was garbled in the source transcript and is omitted; the Sixth Circuit docket number was not stated. Built from the opinion as retrieved and summarized, not a line-by-line read, so verify quotations against the slip opinion. The officer's name was rendered as both Reinink and Rehnink in the source; Reinink is used here. The Supreme Court denied review in Reinink v. Hart on May 26, 2026, covered in the Supreme Court section. Here are the facts. On May 30, 2020, during a demonstration in Grand Rapids that had turned violent, Sean Hart walked toward a police line with his left hand in his pocket. He was unarmed. Sergeant Bush pepper sprayed him from eight to ten feet away. Hart turned, retreated, and began turning back. Officer Reinink then left the line and fired a Speed Heat canister at him at close range, striking his left shoulder. Hart suffered an abrasion and bruising. The detail that makes this a training case: Muzzle Blast and Speed Heat fire from the same 40mm launcher, and the cartridges look similar. Muzzle Blast is a powder dispersion round meant for crowd control. Speed Heat is a tear gas canister designed to be launched into an area, not directly at a person. Reinink said he believed he had loaded Muzzle Blast. The opinion says he loaded without a witness, contrary to policy, and that his body camera was off. The department sustained an excessive force charge against him and suspended him two days without pay. Hart also sued the city, Sergeant Bush, and an officer who pointed a launcher at a car. The holding. The Sixth Circuit affirmed qualified immunity for the officer who pointed a launcher at the car and for Sergeant Bush on the pepper spray, finding no clearly established law against either on those facts. It affirmed summary judgment for the city, because a spreadsheet of about 90 excessive force complaints with generic entries lacked the detail to show a pattern, and the failure to train theory was treated as abandoned. It reversed as to Reinink. Because his own training and his supervisors said Speed Heat could be deadly, and because intent is irrelevant under Graham, the court viewed the evidence in Hart's favor and treated a close range Speed Heat shot as deadly force. The court reasoned that it is the nature of the force, not the label on the weapon, that matters. Against an unarmed man who was not advancing and was eight to ten feet away, a jury could find that force excessive, and the court held that was clearly established in May 2020. On the dissent. Judge Larson concurred in part and dissented in part. She would have granted immunity to Reinink too. She argued that the plaintiff has the burden of identifying a case on point, that the case relied on involved a man who had surrendered with raised hands, and that Hart, by contrast, had been advancing and refusing to comply. She also noted that one case the plaintiff cited was unpublished and cannot clearly establish law. What was not decided. The Supreme Court did not answer whether the officer's intended level of force matters in choosing between the deadly force standard and the general force standard, or whether an officer who used more force than he intended can still claim qualified immunity if his mistake was reasonable. It denied certiorari on May 26, 2026. What this means in practice. In the Sixth Circuit, the force is judged by what the munition could do at that range, not by what the officer meant to fire. An honest mistake about the munition does not change how a court analyzes the shot; the classification follows the weapon, the range, and the target area. The unwitnessed load, the body camera that was off, and the sustained internal affairs finding all appear in the factual record. The city won here because the complaint spreadsheet was too generic and the failure to train theory was abandoned, but a differently pleaded case may not come out the same way. How to train it. Load verification is a use of force skill. An officer issued a launcher that can fire both a crowd control round and a round that can injure badly at close range must be able to identify the round by sight and by procedure every time. Teach the witnessed load as a safety control, not paperwork; the policy that required a second person to witness the load was violated here, and that violation is in the record. For command staff: audit munition storage and issue, separate crowd control rounds from rounds not meant to be fired at people with distinct colors, markings, and containers, and require the witnessed load. Audit body camera activation for civil disturbance deployments. Review how internal affairs findings are drafted, because a sustained excessive force finding appears in the factual record, and review training records, because a failure to train theory that is properly pleaded will be tested against them.

Now the application, the part to take into a briefing room or a training block this week.

First, Armed is not enough, and waiting for the muzzle is not required. Teach officers to articulate the weapon's position, the person's movement, the commands given, and the call history. Handy turned on a shotgun waved overhead by a man walking toward four officers after a report of shots; Ruffin turned on a gun that never moved.

Second, Know which circuit binds you. Handy is binding in the Ninth Circuit and persuasive elsewhere; Ruffin is binding in the Fourth Circuit and persuasive elsewhere. Agencies in other circuits should check their own circuit law before training to either.

Third, Capture the last second. Rewrite report templates and interview protocols to record what the officer saw and what changed between shots, not what the officer concluded the person intended. Ruffin shows how an account of a head turn can be set against the undisputed facts viewed in the plaintiff's favor.

Fourth, Audit less lethal munitions and body cameras. Audit munition identification, storage, and witnessed loading, and audit body camera use in civil disturbances. In Hart, an unwitnessed load and a camera that was off are both in the record, and the sustained internal finding followed the shot into the lawsuit.
