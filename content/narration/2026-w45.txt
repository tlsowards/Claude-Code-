Johnson versus Adgar. Ninth Circuit (from the Northern District of California, Judge Beth Labson Freeman), decided November 2, 2026, docket number 25-1392 and 25-5468. This is a published decision. A verification note before the facts. The opinion was read in full from the official database. The companion order certifying the Bane Act question to the California Supreme Court was not read; the question is quoted from the court staff summary filed with the opinion. Verify every quotation against the slip opinion before using it in a report or in testimony. Here are the facts. San Jose, May 30, 2020, the evening after a night of protests one officer called the most violent and destructive he had seen in fifteen years. A crowd at the City Hall plaza had been generally benign through the day, chanting and singing in front of a police skirmish line. Kyle Johnson arrived at 9:53 p.m. with no sign, no weapon, and nothing to throw, and never engaged with the police. Officer James Adgar arrived at 10:13 p.m. with a 40mm projectile impact weapon, which fires a foam baton round the court described as able to cause serious injury or death. He was posted behind the skirmish line and ordered to shoot anyone he saw throwing bottles. At 10:24 p.m. some protesters threw bottles, some of them glass, at the officers. Officers, including Adgar, fired their launchers and the crowd, including Johnson, retreated, then quickly returned. At 10:33 p.m. a second volley was thrown and officers fired again. Johnson, who had thrown nothing, was hit in the back of his left leg. Doctors later diagnosed a pulmonary embolism attributed to the leg injury. The parties agreed Adgar did not intentionally target Johnson. A jury found for Johnson against Adgar on every claim, found for the City on every claim, awarded $1,353,313 in a single undivided sum, and specifically found that Adgar fired indiscriminately into the crowd. The district court denied Adgar's post-verdict motion for judgment as a matter of law on the Fourth Amendment claims. The holding. Reversed in part, vacated, and remanded, in an opinion by Judge Tallman. First, Johnson was seized. Under Nelson versus City of Davis, a person in a group that officers intentionally fire into is seized when the round limits his movement, even though no one aimed at him. Video showed Johnson running, then limping to cover behind a low wall, and Adgar admitted he fired on purpose. Adgar's innocent bystander argument, that he aimed at a bottle thrower and missed, depended on his version of the facts, and the jury's version controls when substantial evidence supports it. Adgar testified he tried to aim at an individual and pulled the trigger once he believed he had identified him, and could not see whether he hit his target; the jury could reasonably find he never had a clear target and shot in the general direction of the thrower. Second, the force was unreasonable. Johnson was committing no crime, posed no threat, and was not resisting or fleeing arrest, since no one was being arrested. The crowd he was in was retreating and contained at most two bad actors. Given the disproportionate number of peaceful people in the part of the crowd Adgar fired into, there was a significant risk of hitting someone not engaged in wrongdoing with a weapon that could cause serious injury or death. The court said the government's legitimate interest in disabling an aggressor is not reasonably served by firing indiscriminately into a crowd and hoping to hit him. Third, the law was not clearly established, so Adgar gets qualified immunity. The parties agreed Nelson was the relevant precedent. In Nelson the group fired on was entirely peaceful, isolated in a narrow breezeway, and had thrown nothing. Here hostile actors had repeatedly thrown objects, two of them were in the part of the crowd Adgar fired into, that part was not physically isolated, and Adgar fired after a second volley, which suggested the throwers could reload and return. The court relied on its 2024 decision in Puente versus City of Phoenix, where a protester hit while walking away from a mixed crowd lost on the same ground, and described Sanderlin versus Dwyer, where an officer deliberately shot a peaceful man holding a sign, as less relevant. It cited the Supreme Court's repeated warning, including Kisela versus Hughes, that precedent must squarely govern the specific facts. On the dissent. Judge Paez agreed there was a constitutional violation but would have denied immunity. He wrote that Nelson clearly established the unlawfulness of shooting a projectile weapon capable of causing serious and permanent injury indiscriminately into a group of protesters who did not pose an imminent risk to public safety. He said the majority viewed the evidence in the light favorable to the officer instead of the jury's winner. The evidence about where the bottles came from was at best mixed: a video forensic expert placed the throwers relatively far from Johnson, and Johnson saw no one near him throw anything. He also wrote that the majority could not use Puente, decided four years after the shooting, to say the law was unclear in 2020, and that the majority had reversed the logic of Tan Lam versus City of Los Banos, which allows later cases that found a violation of clearly established law to be considered, not later cases that found immunity. What was not decided. The Bane Act claim. The panel held that a Bane Act claim has the same elements as a section 1983 claim plus specific intent, so the constitutional violation it found carries over, but it did not decide whether Adgar violated a clearly delineated and plainly applicable right under state law. It certified that question to the California Supreme Court, told the district court not to recalculate attorney fees until the panel decides the state claim, and held the mandate. The jury's battery and negligence verdicts were not challenged on appeal. What this means in practice. A launcher round that hits the wrong person is not an accident outside the Fourth Amendment. If the officer fired on purpose at a group, everyone in the group is an object of that force. The jury's finding that the fire was indiscriminate decided this case, and the court said it did not matter that Adgar had been ordered to shoot only bottle throwers if the jury could find he did not follow that order. The officer won immunity only because no earlier case involved a crowd that had itself thrown objects at police. That protection is gone for the next Ninth Circuit officer who fires a launcher into a mixed, retreating crowd, because this opinion is now the case on point. In California, a federal immunity win may not end the exposure if the California Supreme Court reads the Bane Act standard differently. How to train it. Teach the 40mm launcher as a point target tool, never an area tool. The articulation for every round is a named person doing a specific act, the distance, the expected point of impact, and who is behind and beside the target. If the operator cannot identify the target, the operator holds. Use a spotter or team leader to designate the target out loud before the launch, which separates the decision from the trigger and creates a record. Train that a second volley from a few people is not a blank check for area fire at everyone; it helped this officer on notice, not on reasonableness. Train supervisors to document the whole picture, including the hours of peaceful protest, the skirmish line, and the composition of the crowd, because the totality is the test. For command staff: a policy that leans on qualified immunity is written for the lawyers, not the street. Train to the first prong. Review less lethal policy language so impact launchers are separated from area effect dispersal tools, and brief the city attorney and risk manager on the Bane Act certification now.

Astorga versus Villanueva. Ninth Circuit (from the Central District of California), decided October 21, 2026, docket number 24-4061. This decision is unpublished, so it is not binding precedent. A verification note before the facts. This memorandum could not be read from an official source for this brief. Everything here comes from press coverage, including the Metropolitan News-Enterprise, the Davis Vanguard, and Bloomberg Law. It is unpublished and not precedent. Confirm it with counsel before relying on any detail. It was filed the week before this brief's window and is included as a companion to Johnson. Here are the facts. As reported, Christina Astorga and Hugo Padilla alleged that Los Angeles County sheriff's deputies fired less lethal projectiles at them as they retreated after an unlawful assembly declaration at a 2020 protest in South Los Angeles, striking and injuring them. They sued former Sheriff Alex Villanueva on a supervisory liability theory. The district court denied him qualified immunity. The holding. Reversed, as reported, in an unpublished memorandum by a panel of Judges R. Nelson and Desai and a visiting district judge. The plaintiffs did not identify an earlier decision establishing that the sheriff's own supervisory conduct violated their rights. The panel distinguished Larez versus City of Los Angeles, the Ninth Circuit's chief-of-police case, noting that Villanueva had not dismissed complaints the way the chief there did. What was not decided. Whether the deputies' conduct on the line was constitutional. The memorandum, as reported, addressed only the sheriff's notice as a supervisor. What this means in practice. The deputies at the front face one analysis. The command staff who set policy and supervise face another, and the notice requirement is harder for a plaintiff to meet at the top of the chain. That is not comfort. It means the documented decisions about authorization, supervision, complaint handling, and after-action review are the record that will be tested. How to train it. For command staff, keep the paper. Authorization for less lethal deployment, who was designated to give it, what the supervision looked like during the event, how complaints were handled afterward, and what changed as a result. Larez turned on a chief who brushed off complaints; the sheriff here was distinguished because he did not.

Now, the California. No published California force decision was found this week. The California item is the question the Ninth Circuit sent to the California Supreme Court in Johnson v. Adgar. The Tom Bane Civil Rights Act, Civil Code section 52.1, gives a state remedy when someone interferes with constitutional rights by threat, intimidation, or coercion. Under Cornell v. City and County of San Francisco, a 2017 Court of Appeal decision, the right must be clearly delineated and plainly applicable under the circumstances. The certified question, as stated in the court's summary, is: what is the standard for determining whether a particular constitutional right is clearly delineated and plainly applicable under the Bane Act, and what is the difference, if any, between that standard and the clearly established standard for federal qualified immunity. If the answer is that the state standard is different, a California officer could win federal immunity, as Adgar did, and still face state liability for the same force. As of this brief there is no indication the California Supreme Court has acted on the request; it decides whether to accept certified questions, and timing estimates in legal commentary are estimates, not deadlines.

Now the application, the part to take into a briefing room or a training block this week.

First, Aim at a person, not at a crowd. The key fact in Johnson was not that bottles were thrown. It was the jury's finding that the officer fired indiscriminately. Teach the 40mm launcher as a point target tool: if the operator cannot name the person and the act that justify this round, there is no articulation.

Second, Designate the target before the launch. Use a spotter or team leader to call the target out loud, check the backdrop, and hold fire when the line of fire includes people who have done nothing. That separates the decision from the trigger and creates the record a jury will believe.

Third, A second volley is not a blank check. The majority gave the second volley weight on notice, which is a legal question about what earlier cases said. It did nothing to make the force reasonable. Do not teach that repeated throwing by a few people authorizes area fire at everyone.

Fourth, Train to the first prong. The court held the force unconstitutional. The officer won only because no earlier case was specific enough, and this opinion is now that case for the Ninth Circuit. Qualified immunity is a defense in court, not a tactic on the street, and a policy that cites it is written for the lawyers.

Fifth, In California, brief counsel on the Bane Act certification now. If the California Supreme Court says the state standard differs from federal qualified immunity, a federal immunity win will not end the exposure. Ask counsel whether policies and training records are organized to meet a state standard that may turn out to be stricter.

Sixth, Command staff, keep the paper. Astorga shows that liability at the top of the chain is hard for a plaintiff to prove, and that the record of authorization, supervision, complaint handling, and after-action review is what gets tested. Document those decisions as they are made.
