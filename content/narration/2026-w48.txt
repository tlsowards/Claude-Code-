This is the Use of Force Case Law Update from Sowards Consulting, for the week of November 22 through 28, 2026. This update is written for trainers, supervisors, and the officers they train, in patrol, probation, custody, and juvenile settings. The order is always the same: federal law first, starting with the Ninth Circuit and the Supreme Court, then any other federal circuit or California decision that matters, and then application, meaning what to do with it on the street, in the jail, and in the juvenile hall. Every case ends with two things: what it means in practice, and how to train it.

Here is the short version. The Ninth Circuit's full court decided King v. Villegas on November 24: a Kern Valley State Prison inmate who pleaded no contest to resisting an officer may still sue the two correctional officers for excessive force, because a no contest plea admits no facts and success on the force claim would not necessarily imply the conviction was invalid. The court overruled part of Sanders v. City of Pittsburg. Six judges joined the opinion, three concurred in the judgment only and warned the holding could keep Heck v. Humphrey from applying in no contest cases, and two dissented, one of them closing with "So long, Heck." Elsewhere, the Fifth Circuit held in Berg v. Rivas that a San Antonio officer's six second spotlight on a bystander's phone camera and a second officer's perimeter around a traffic stop were not enough to support a First Amendment retaliation claim, while confirming that recording the police is protected activity. The Seventh Circuit held in McNamara v. King that pepper spraying a Wisconsin prisoner who was standing on his sink and harming himself was a good faith effort to restore order, not excessive force. The Supreme Court issued no force decision or order this holiday week, and no California published decision addressed force.

Now, the Ninth Circuit. One decision, and it is the biggest custody force ruling from this court in some time: the full eleven judge court sat on King versus Villegas and split three ways over whether a prisoner's no contest plea to resisting an officer bars his excessive force suit under Heck versus Humphrey. The court's two other custody decisions this week are procedural. Ewalan versus Washington Department of Corrections vacated a defense verdict in a failure to protect case because the district court should have appointed substitute counsel for a prisoner with a traumatic brain injury, over a dissent by Judge Bumatay, and Murillo versus Castillo is about grievance exhaustion and joinder at Salinas Valley State Prison. Neither decides anything about force, and neither is covered further.

King versus Villegas. Ninth Circuit, en banc (from the Eastern District of California, Judge Jennifer L. Thurston), decided November 24, 2026, docket number 23-1713. This is a published decision. Here are the facts. On August 17, 2016, Jerry Lee King, newly transferred to Kern Valley State Prison and on orientation status, was being escorted with about nine other new arrivals to another building by Officer R. Villegas and a second officer. The two accounts diverge from there. King's pro se complaint says the inmates were handcuffed, that an inmate behind him asked a question and he answered, and that Villegas approached and said, "What don't you understand about shut your effing mouth?" King replied that Villegas did not have to get in his face and talk to him that way. Then, "without any provocation," Villegas grabbed King by the back of his shirt and "with great force" "rammed King's head into the wall" with his forearm, splitting his head open. King said "what the eff," and Villegas swept his feet, took him to the ground, and yelled a code one assault on staff. Officer P. Cruz responded and, "while King was lying on the ground," "struck him with great force and a closed fist in his right eye" and ground his ankles into the asphalt. King alleged a sergeant later told medical staff to document that he had "refused treatment." He was placed in solitary confinement. The officers' incident reports say King was not handcuffed, was facing the wall and talking loudly while other inmates checked in, was told to be quiet, and then "without warning or provocation" swung both arms upward, twisted, and struck Villegas's right shoulder with his right hand, continuing to twist and try to elbow him. Villegas pushed King toward the wall to secure him in handcuffs, which caused King's head to strike the wall. Cruz forced King to the ground in a prone position, Villegas landed on top of him, King kept struggling, Villegas announced a Code 1 staff battery by radio, Cruz held his legs, and King then stopped and complied with orders to put his hands behind his back. All three were medically examined and another officer took photographs; the court noted that "no photographs are in the record on appeal." A prison disciplinary hearing found King guilty of battery on a peace officer. King filed his section 1983 suit in May 2017. Several months later, and more than a year after the incident, Kern County charged him with battery by a prisoner on a non-confined person and with resisting an executive officer under Penal Code section 69; the federal case was stayed. The criminal case took nearly five years. In March 2022 King entered a no contest plea under People versus West to the section 69 count for an eight month sentence, and the battery count was dismissed. At the plea hearing the judge described the plea as "I don't really admit" and "kind of like no contest on steroids," King said "I don't want my plea to affect my civil case in any way," his lawyer said the West plea "is intended so that it won't impact the civil suit" but could not promise that, and counsel stipulated to a factual basis "based on the probable cause statement and the reports in discovery with the understanding this is a People vs. West and a no contest plea." The officers then moved for judgment on the pleadings under Heck. A magistrate judge and the first district judge denied the motion; after reassignment, Judge Thurston granted it, finding "there is no possible way these two sets of facts could simultaneously be true." A three judge panel reversed on Federal Rule of Evidence 410 grounds, and the court took the case en banc. The holding. Reversed and remanded, in an opinion by Judge W. Fletcher. Heck bars a civil claim only where a favorable judgment would "necessarily imply the invalidity of a conviction or sentence," and the court began from common ground: "success on a civil excessive force claim against an officer under section 1983 is not necessarily inconsistent with a valid conviction for resisting that officer," because "the officer used too much force to respond to the assault or resistance or that the officer used force after the need for force had disappeared." Under California law a section 69 or section 148 conviction requires that the officer was acting lawfully, so a force claim is barred "if the force that the plaintiff challenges as unlawful is the same force that the plaintiff was convicted of resisting." The court's earlier en banc cases, Lemos and Smith versus City of Hemet, involved a jury verdict and a guilty plea, where facts were proven or admitted, and the court looked at the record to see which acts formed the basis of the conviction. A no contest plea is different. It "is a special creature under the law" and is "first and foremost, not an admission of factual guilt." The question, the court said, "is whether, in a civil suit under section 1983, a plaintiff who pleaded nolo to a state criminal charge will be bound to a factual narrative that was never proven and whose truth he never admitted. As we explain below, the answer is no." Because King's "guilt was neither proven nor admitted," "there is therefore no factual narrative to which King is bound," and "to the extent that the three-judge panel opinion in Sanders versus City of Pittsburg, 14 F fourth 968 , can be read as holding otherwise, it is overruled." The conviction still presupposes a prima facie factual basis for the charge, so the claim would be barred if success were "logically incompatible with the existence of a prima facie basis for the criminal charge against him." It was not. The district court had described King as alleging he was "not resisting the entire time," but "King did not write the words quoted in the brief"; he alleged he was not resisting at one point, when Cruz struck him in the eye, and wrote that he never provoked the officers. "Never resisting (as Defendants-Appellees would have it) and never provoking (as King wrote) are distinct." A factfinder could find excessive force at another point, "for example, when Villegas allegedly 'rammed King's head into the wall,' or when Cruz was allegedly 'grinding the Plaintiff's ankles' into the ground," because resistance "may precede or follow the excessive use of force by that same officer even within 'a single continuous chain of events lasting a very brief time.'" The court added a limit: "We do not hold that the Heck bar will never apply when a conviction is based on a nolo plea under Penal Code section 1192.5." It did not reach Rule 410. Judge Owens, joined by Judges Miller and Forrest, concurred in the judgment only. He would have let King amend his complaint to say which acts of force and which officer he is suing over, then had the district court run the Lemos analysis, and he warned that "if left unchecked, the majority's holding could effectively prevent Heck from applying in nolo cases. This is a dramatic change in how judges have applied this doctrine for decades and will have significant ramifications in our federal and state trial courts." On the dissent. Judge Collins, joined by Judge VanDyke and joined as to his first two parts by Judges Owens, Miller, and Forrest, would have affirmed. In his view the majority's account of California no contest pleas is "profoundly wrong": Penal Code section 1016(3) makes the legal effect of a no contest plea to a felony punishable offense "the same as that of a plea of guilty for all purposes," the California Supreme Court has called the contrary argument "specious," and a People versus West plea "simply means that the plea was the result of a plea bargain." The factual basis here was counsel's stipulation to the probable cause statement and the reports, which "cover all of the resistive conduct described by the officers over the course of the entire incident," so all of the officers' conduct during that resistance was lawful for Heck purposes and every one of King's allegations is inconsistent with it. To call the difference between never provoking and never resisting "a technical difference," he wrote, "is to read the complaint casuistically rather than reasonably." Judge VanDyke, dissenting separately, called the case "a tale of two stories" and said the court has developed a "slice-and-dice" approach that lets a judge "transform one continuous officer-defendant incident into a theoretically limitless number of sub-incidents." King's complaint, he wrote, "tells a tale of unlawful conduct from start to finish," so the majority had to rest on a hypothetical that King resisted at some other point, a possibility that "exists only if you turn on 'track changes' and redline King's complaint for him." He cited the Fifth, Seventh, and Tenth Circuits and the California Supreme Court for the rule that a plaintiff who pleads a wholly unprovoked attack pleads into the Heck bar, and he closed: "So long, Heck. It's been nice knowing you." What was not decided. Whether any force was excessive. The case was decided on the pleadings, the officers' reports tell a different story, and the court took King's allegations as true because it had to. Whether Rule 410 bars a court from considering a no contest plea and the plea hearing for Heck purposes; the panel had said yes, and the en banc court did not reach it. Whether Heck can ever bar a claim after a no contest plea; the court said it was not holding that the bar never applies, only that it does not apply here. What California law makes of a West plea in a state court civil suit; the court said that is a state law question it was not deciding. Whether King's prison disciplinary finding has any effect on the suit. What this means in practice. For custody staff, the headline is simple: in the Ninth Circuit, a prisoner's no contest plea to resisting staff no longer closes the civil case over the same incident. Officers and agencies that have treated a criminal disposition as the end of the civil exposure should stop doing so for no contest pleas, and the same reasoning reaches a street arrest that ends in a no contest plea to section 69 or section 148. What follows from that is about reports. King's officers wrote reports that became the factual basis for the plea, and those reports will now be tested against his complaint in front of a factfinder. The majority's analysis works moment by moment: resistance at one point, force at another, and the question is whether the force at each point was lawful. The dissents call that slicing and dicing, but it is the law of the circuit, and the report that survives it is the one that describes the sequence in order with the resistance that preceded each application of force and the point at which the resistance stopped. The officers' reports here did that in outline: strikes to the shoulder, an attempt to elbow, a push to the wall, a takedown to prone, continued struggling, a radio call, legs controlled, hands behind the back, cuffs, relief by other staff. The second practical point is the record that was not there. The court noted that photographs were taken and that none were in the record on appeal, and King alleged a sergeant told medical staff to document a refusal of treatment. Injury photographs of the inmate and of staff, the medical evaluations, and any refusal documented in the inmate's own words are the evidence that answers an allegation like that one. Third, the plea hearing itself is instructive. King asked twice whether the plea would affect his civil case, the judge said he did not know, and defense counsel said the West plea was intended not to affect it. Prosecutors and agency liaisons should understand that a stipulation to the reports as the factual basis for a no contest plea does not bind the plaintiff to those reports in federal court. Finally, read the vote. Six judges joined the opinion, three joined only the judgment and said the holding "could effectively prevent Heck from applying in nolo cases," and five judges joined the parts of the Collins dissent that call the majority's reading of California plea law wrong. Expect the losing side to seek further review, and expect district courts to follow the majority in the meantime. How to train it. Teach the rule in one sentence: a resisting conviction and an excessive force claim can both be true, and a no contest plea admits no facts at all. Pair King with Smith versus City of Hemet from the Case library, where the full court said the same thing about a guilty plea under section 148, so staff hear that this is not new; what is new is that a no contest plea no longer carries a factual narrative into the civil case. Then train the report. Walk through the officers' account in King as a sequence and have staff mark every point where force was applied and name the resistance that preceded it; then mark the point where the inmate complied and the force changed to control and handcuffing. That is the report that survives a moment by moment review. Train the evidence package after a staff assault: photographs of the inmate and of staff, the medical evaluations, and any refusal of treatment recorded in the inmate's own words, with the inmate's signature where policy allows. Teach supervisors how King's complaint used the medical record: an allegation that a sergeant told medical staff what to write is answered by a record that shows who wrote it and when. For patrol trainers, run the same lesson with a section 148 arrest: the no contest plea the prosecutor offers to clear the case does not end the civil suit, and the report is still the officer's best evidence. Teach the posture accurately: the case was decided on the pleadings, nothing has been found about the force, and the court said Heck can still bar some claims after a no contest plea.

Now, the Other federal circuits. Two unpublished decisions, neither binding precedent, each useful in a briefing. Berg is a bystander recording case from a San Antonio traffic stop that turns on the difference between a six second spotlight and a continuous obstruction of the camera. McNamara is a Wisconsin prison case about pepper spray used on a prisoner who was standing on his sink and harming himself, decided at the screening stage under the Hudson versus McMillian good faith standard.

Berg versus Rivas. Fifth Circuit (from the Western District of Texas), decided November 24, 2026, docket number 26-50184. This decision is unpublished, so it is not binding precedent. Here are the facts. On April 11, 2024, at about 10:36 p.m., Kevin Berg and his wife stopped at a San Antonio gas station where a traffic stop was underway. When Berg came out of the store the stop "appeared to have escalated beyond a routine traffic stop," and he began recording on his phone from beside his parked car at a pump, in a well lit area. Officer Thomas Rivas stepped away from the stop and confronted him: "Hey, bro. Can you get outta here? Huh? Can you get outta here? Can you get outta here, or no?" When Berg asked why Rivas was talking to him, Rivas said, "Why are you recording? Why are you recording? You must have no effing life, right? Get out of here, dude." Rivas assigned another officer to stand by Berg and watch him. Several minutes later Rivas turned his vehicle spotlight onto Berg's camera for about six seconds; Berg moved "almost immediately" and kept recording from another spot. As he moved, Officer Manuel Gandara told him, "if you get near this traffic stop, you're gonna go to jail," and repeated twice that he would go to jail if he got closer. Berg stood in place and filmed for nearly the rest of the recording. He complained to the department two days later; a sergeant told him the only issue identified was Rivas's profanity, and after a demand letter a captain told him Rivas had been "reprimanded for language." Berg sued pro se under section 1983 for First Amendment retaliation against both officers and for Monell liability against the city. His complaint also alleged that other people, two men on foot, a red car at an adjacent pump, and a man who walked between the detained truck and the patrol car, came as close or closer without being told to leave. The district court dismissed everything and denied his motion to alter the judgment. The holding. Affirmed, per curiam, by Judges Ho, Engelhardt, and Wilson. The first element of a retaliation claim was met: the district court "correctly determined that Berg's recording was constitutionally protected activity" under the circuit's Turner versus Lieutenant Driver decision. The case ended on the second element, whether the officers' actions "caused Berg to suffer an injury that would chill a person of ordinary firmness from continuing to engage in that activity." The court explained that the effect on speech "need not be great" to be actionable, but "some retaliatory actions—even if they actually have the effect of chilling the plaintiff's speech—are too trivial or minor to be actionable." The spotlight "curtailed Berg's speech, but 'trivially' so," and the perimeter "did not curtail Berg's speech at all." On the spotlight, the court compared its own unpublished decision in Rincon versus City of Laredo, where a six second confiscation of a phone was not enough, and distinguished Perkins versus Hart, where a deputy "moved from side to side to block" a recording, and the Tenth Circuit's Irizarry versus Yehia, where a flashlight "saturated the camera sensors" and made recording all but impossible. Rivas "did not move the spotlight from side to side to create a continuous obstruction"; "he turned it on, and Berg promptly moved aside. That distinction makes a difference." On the perimeter: it "simply delineated the area where he could continue to do so," and holding otherwise "would contort the right to film police, as expressed in Turner, into an entitlement to film officers from whatever angle and distance a plaintiff pleases. But the right to film the police 'is not without limitations.' Like all speech, filming the police 'may be subject to reasonable time, place, and manner restrictions.'" Berg's "completely uninterrupted recording following the alleged adverse conduct at least suggests that Rivas's conduct would not have chilled the speech of a person of ordinary firmness." With no constitutional violation, the officers had qualified immunity and the Monell claim against the city failed. The allegations that other bystanders were allowed closer could go only to retaliatory motive, which the court did not reach. What was not decided. Whether the officers acted with retaliatory motive; the court stopped at the injury element. Whether Rivas's profanity, his order to leave, and his assignment of an officer to watch Berg would have been enough in combination; the court said the complaint pleaded only the order to leave and the spotlight as Rivas's adverse actions, and that Berg offered no case making the rest sufficient. Whether the body camera footage could be considered at the pleading stage; the court said it did not matter. Anything about the underlying traffic stop or any use of force. What this means in practice. Three things officers can take from Berg, in order of importance. First, the recording was protected. The Fifth Circuit said so in the first paragraph of its analysis, and the Ninth Circuit recognizes the same right to record officers in public places. A bystander with a phone at a gas pump, in a well lit area, outside the stop, is exercising a right, and the question is only how officers manage the scene around that right. Second, the line the court drew is between a momentary interference and an obstruction. Six seconds of spotlight that the man stepped out of was trivial; a flashlight that blinds the camera, or an officer who moves side to side to block it, has been enough in other courts. The practical rule is simple: do not point lights at cameras and do not block them. Third, a perimeter is a time, place, and manner restriction, and it held up here because it applied to the recording itself only in the sense that the man could not come closer. What the court did not reach is the allegation that other people were allowed closer without being told to leave. That allegation goes to motive, and in a case that clears the injury element it is the allegation that would decide the case. A perimeter that is enforced against the person with the camera and not against anyone else is the fact pattern to avoid. Finally, the words. "You must have no effing life, right?" earned a reprimand and a place in a federal opinion. The department's internal review found that the profanity was the only problem, and the court's result agrees, but the recording is the reason everyone knows what was said. How to train it. Train scene management with a recording bystander as a standard element of traffic stop scenarios: the trainee sets a perimeter, states it once in plain terms, applies it to everyone, and keeps lights and bodies off the camera. Debrief the two officers in Berg separately. Rivas's approach, the profanity, the order to leave, and the spotlight are the conduct that produced a complaint and a lawsuit; Gandara's perimeter warning, stated three times without profanity, is the conduct the court called a reasonable restriction. Teach the difference the court drew between a six second spotlight and a continuous obstruction, and teach that other courts have found a blinding flashlight or a body used to block a camera sufficient to state a claim. For supervisors, use the complaint handling as a second lesson: a sergeant's finding that profanity was the only issue, and a captain's letter that the officer had been reprimanded for language, were the department's record in the federal case. Teach the posture: unpublished, decided on the complaint, and silent on motive.

McNamara versus King. Seventh Circuit (from the Eastern District of Wisconsin), decided November 23, 2026, docket number 26-1111. This decision is unpublished, so it is not binding precedent. Here are the facts. In August 2025, Sean McNamara, a prisoner at Green Bay Correctional Institution, began harming himself after learning that staff had searched his cell while he was away. He told an officer he had self-harmed, showed the blood on his arm, and asked for medical attention; the officer called for help. While waiting outside his cell he tapped small metal fragments from his window frame and swallowed them, along with a rubber pencil. Officer Marcus King arrived within minutes and took him to the health services unit, where staff treated his arm. McNamara asked to be sent offsite to have the metal removed; King refused, and McNamara described him as "very angry." McNamara was placed on observation status in restricted housing. A couple of hours later a doctor saw him by telehealth; he asked for a blanket because he was cold, and the doctor said she would consult King. Two hours after that King passed his cell, McNamara asked about the blanket, and King said, "yes maybe no, we will see how you act tonight." McNamara, who thought King was "messing with him," replied, "you must want me to freak out and hurt myself," climbed onto his sink, about three feet high, and began to harm himself again. King yelled at him, drew a taser and pointed it, and sent Officer David Johnson for pepper spray, instructing him to spray McNamara "if McNamara looked like he was going to jump off the sink or otherwise start to self-harm." McNamara began to harm himself while standing on the sink, and Johnson sprayed him in the eyes and the right side of his body. He could not see and fell off the sink, climbed back up, considered jumping so he could hit his head on the bed, decided not to, started to climb down, slipped, and fell again. Minutes later he was handcuffed and escorted down the hall, and another officer remarked, "wow that's a lot of spray." He sued King and Johnson under the Eighth Amendment and Wisconsin tort law. The district court dismissed the complaint at screening for failure to state a claim, declined supplemental jurisdiction over the state claims, and denied leave to amend as futile, including a later motion to add two unnamed officers for failure to intervene. The holding. Affirmed, in a nonprecedential order by Judges Sykes, Jackson-Akiwumi, and Taibleson. The court applied the Hudson standard: "whenever prison officials stand accused of using excessive physical force in violation of the Cruel and Unusual Punishments Clause, the core judicial inquiry is . . . whether force was applied in a good-faith effort to maintain or restore discipline, or maliciously and sadistically to cause harm." McNamara's "thorough characterization of the events does not allow a reasonable inference that the officers applied force for any reason other than to restore order to a situation that had grown alarming." The court pointed to his own allegations: "that he climbed up on the sink and began to harm himself, that King directed Johnson to use the pepper spray only if McNamara looked like he was about to jump or harm himself, and that only after he harmed himself did Johnson use the spray." Because his self-harm posed a risk to his safety, "the officers' use of pepper spray was not an 'unnecessary . . . infliction of pain' but a good-faith intervention to deescalate the situation and restore order." The court also upheld the denial of leave to amend, because McNamara did not say what new allegations would support a claim. What was not decided. Whether the officers' account would match the prisoner's; they were never served and did not appear. Whether the amount of spray was excessive; the remark "wow that's a lot of spray" is in the complaint, and the court did not treat it as changing the analysis. Whether a failure to intervene claim against the unnamed officers would have survived; the court held only that the motion to add them came after judgment and did not explain how it would change the result. Any medical deliberate indifference claim about the swallowed metal, the refused offsite transport, or the blanket. The state law claims, which were dismissed without prejudice. What this means in practice. McNamara is a custody and juvenile hall case about the hardest kind of force decision: force used to stop a person from hurting himself. The court approved it under Hudson because the prisoner's own account showed three things. The instruction to use the spray was conditional, tied to a specific behavior, jumping or renewed self-harm. The spray was used only when that behavior resumed. And the force stopped there; he was handcuffed and escorted minutes later. Those are the facts that made the force a "good-faith intervention," and they are the facts a report in a self-harm response should contain. Two cautions. First, the exchange that preceded the escalation is also in the opinion. A blanket request routed through the doctor to the officer, an answer of "yes maybe no, we will see how you act tonight," and a prisoner who heard it as retaliation for an argument hours earlier is the sequence that put him on the sink. The court did not fault the officer for it, but a different plaintiff with a different record could plead the same words as evidence of malice. Second, the remark "wow that's a lot of spray" did not change this case at the screening stage, where the question was the officers' motive. In a case with a medical record, quantity and decontamination are facts a court will weigh, and the opinion says nothing about either. For juvenile detention, the same Hudson style good faith inquiry informs how courts look at force on a youth in crisis, and the lesson is identical: condition the force on the behavior, use it when the behavior happens, stop when it stops, and get medical staff to the youth. How to train it. Run a self-harm response scenario from this opinion: a prisoner on a fixture three feet off the floor, actively harming himself, who has already swallowed metal earlier in the shift. Train the supervising officer to state the condition out loud before any force, the way King did, so that every officer present and the record know what will trigger the spray and what will not. Train the report to capture the condition, the behavior that met it, the application, and the stop. Then debrief the blanket exchange. Ask officers how "we will see how you act tonight" sounds to a person who has already harmed himself twice that day and has swallowed metal fragments, and offer the alternative: answer the medical question as a medical question and route it back to the doctor. Teach the medical side of the same event as a separate module: the refused offsite transport for swallowed metal and the blanket request were not before the court as deliberate indifference claims, but they are the claims a better pleaded complaint would carry. Teach the posture: a nonprecedential order, decided on the prisoner's own allegations at screening, with the officers never served.

Now the application, the part to take into a briefing room or a training block this week.

First, A no contest plea does not end the civil case. In the Ninth Circuit, after King versus Villegas, a prisoner or arrestee who pleads no contest to resisting under section 69 or section 148 may still sue for excessive force over the same incident, because the plea admits no facts. Agencies should stop treating that disposition as the end of the civil exposure and should assume the officers' reports will be tested against the complaint.

Second, Write the sequence, not the summary. The court's analysis works moment by moment: what resistance preceded each application of force, and when the resistance stopped. The officers' reports in King described strikes, a push to the wall, a takedown, continued struggling, a radio call, leg control, compliance, and cuffs, in order. A report built that way is the one that survives a Heck motion or a trial.

Third, Put the photographs and the medical record in the file. Photographs of the inmate and of staff were taken in King and none were in the record on appeal, while the complaint alleged a sergeant told medical staff to document a refusal of treatment. Injury photographs, both medical evaluations, and any refusal recorded in the inmate's own words are what answer that allegation.

Fourth, Do not block the camera; set a perimeter and apply it to everyone. Recording a traffic stop from a gas pump is protected activity. In Berg a six second spotlight the bystander stepped out of was too trivial to support a claim, but other courts have found a blinding flashlight or an officer moving to block the lens sufficient. A perimeter is a reasonable restriction when it applies to everyone; the unaddressed allegation in Berg was that others were allowed closer.

Fifth, The words are the record. "You must have no effing life, right?" got a San Antonio officer reprimanded and quoted in the Fifth Circuit's Berg opinion. "We will see how you act tonight," in McNamara versus King, is the line a Wisconsin prisoner in crisis heard as retaliation before he climbed onto his sink, and "wow that's a lot of spray," from another officer in the same case, went into the complaint. Say what you would want read aloud in court, because it will be.

Sixth, Self-harm response: condition the force, use it on the behavior, stop when it stops. The pepper spray in McNamara was approved because the instruction was conditional, the spray was used only when the prisoner resumed harming himself, and the force ended with handcuffs minutes later. State the condition out loud, document the behavior that met it, and get medical staff to the person. The opinion says nothing about quantity or decontamination; a record that does will be judged on them.
