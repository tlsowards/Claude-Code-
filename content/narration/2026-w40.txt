Edwards versus Brown. Ninth Circuit (from the District of Oregon), decided September 28, 2026, docket number 25-6849. This is a published decision. Here are the facts. On October 1, 2022, officers at the Oregon State Penitentiary used pepper spray to break up a fight in the cell next to Deymon Edwards. The cells are open-front, and he says the spray reached his face, eyes, skin, and lungs. He asked for a decontamination shower and was denied for about two days. His grievance was rejected on the ground that non-direct exposure does not require a shower or clean clothing. A lieutenant answered that everyone with direct exposure had been removed and decontaminated, and the superintendent found he was only indirectly exposed. The grievance process ended January 13, 2023. He sued under 42 U S C section 1983 on January 10, 2025, and the district court dismissed the case as time barred. The holding. Reversed. Reading Oregon Revised Statutes section 12.210 together with the Prison Litigation Reform Act exhaustion requirement at 42 U S C section 1997 e(a), the court held that the limitations period is tolled while an inmate is exhausting the grievance process. Tolling starts no later than the day the inmate hands in the grievance, not the day the office stamps it received. The first, denied grievance counted because Oregon's rules do not allow a denied grievance to be appealed. That gave 102 days of tolling and made the complaint timely. Panel: Judges Paez, Tallman, and Bennett, opinion by Judge Bennett. No dissent. What was not decided. The court did not decide whether the spray or the two-day denial of decontamination violated the Eighth Amendment, and did not decide whether the decontamination policy was lawful. Qualified immunity was not raised or decided. The court expressly left open whether the clock also stops between the incident and the day the grievance is filed. The tolling statute is Oregon's, so California agencies should not assume the same rule applies. What this means in practice. A pepper spray claim can arrive more than two years after the deployment and still be timely. And a policy line saying non-direct exposure does not require decontamination is now quoted in a published Ninth Circuit opinion that goes back to the trial court on the merits. Whatever happens next, that is the sentence a plaintiff's lawyer will read to a jury. How to train it. Write the decontamination policy around exposure, not around who was the target. After every deployment, staff should ask who else was in the air, including the open-front cell next door and the shared dayroom, and offer and log decontamination for each person. Teach staff not to argue with someone who is coughing and burning about whether they were the intended target. Build retention into policy so deployment reports, decontamination logs, grievances, and video survive well past the nominal two-year window. The operational lesson travels to juvenile facilities that authorize chemical agents: the young person in the next room breathes the same air.

Mason versus Talley. Fourth Circuit (from the Eastern District of Virginia), decided September 28, 2026, docket number 23-7201. This is a published decision. Here are the facts. Omari Mason was a convicted prisoner at Riverside Regional Jail, housed in a high-restriction unit where policy required restraints any time he was out of his cell. On January 7, 2022, he asked to use the phone during his one hour of recreation. Staff secured one hand by a chain to a wall-mounted phone, and he stayed there about six and a half hours. Video confirmed it, and showed he could sit, stand, and move, with periodic checks. About four and a half hours in, he urinated on himself after being denied the bathroom. On January 12, he was chained to the same phone for somewhere between two and five hours. An officer said he would bring water and never did. Mason fainted from dehydration and hit his head. Video was produced for the first day, none for the second. He also challenged about five months in restrictive housing. The holding. Affirmed summary judgment for the officers, two to one. Because Mason was a convicted prisoner, the Eighth Amendment governed. The shackling did not meet the objective prong, which requires an extreme deprivation or serious injury. The majority distinguished Hope versus Pelzer: Hope was chained outdoors to a hitching post in the sun, shirtless, arms above shoulder height, as punishment, and taunted. Mason was indoors and clothed, the restraint came from housing policy, and he could sit and stand. The majority rejected the reading of Hope that securing an inmate to a fixed object without an emergency is automatically unconstitutional. In the alternative, the officers had qualified immunity because the law was not clearly established in January 2022. The due process claim about restrictive housing failed for lack of an atypical and significant hardship, and summary judgment before discovery was not an abuse of discretion. Majority by Judge Rushing, joined by Judge Richardson. On the dissent. Judge Benjamin, concurring in part and dissenting in part, would have sent the shackling claims to a jury and would have found a clearly established violation for prolonged shackling while the man was denied water and the bathroom. She pointed out that the defendants first denied the six-hour restraint ever happened, and changed position only after the court ordered them to produce evidence and the video confirmed it. On the second day, only one officer walked by in nearly four hours, no footage was produced, and the officer who chained Mason allegedly told him afterward not to say anything. What was not decided. The majority did not say chaining a person to a fixed object is acceptable. It said these facts, as it read them, did not meet the Eighth Amendment threshold, and that in any event the law was not clearly established. The analysis turned on Mason's status as a convicted prisoner. The court did not decide how the same facts would come out for a pretrial detainee or a juvenile, where the Fourteenth Amendment and the objective reasonableness standard of Kingsley versus Hendrickson apply. What this means in practice. Read this as a warning, not an approval. The officers won by one vote, and the facts the majority relied on, indoors, clothed, able to move, periodic checks, are exactly what separates this case from Hope. Take those away and the dissent's road map is waiting for the next plaintiff. The evidence problem is just as important: missing video for the second day and an early denial that the incident happened are what the dissent built on. How to train it. If a facility ever secures a person to a fixed object, policy needs a stated reason, a time limit, scheduled checks at a set interval, access to water and a toilet, and documentation of every one of those. A check is an assessment that gets logged, not a glance through a window. If a one-hour recreation restraint runs past the hour, a supervisor is notified. Nobody is chained and left without a relief plan. Preserve the video for every incident. Train candor: if an incident happened, the report says it happened, and nobody denies in litigation what the camera recorded. Juvenile and pretrial facilities should not treat Mason as permission. Talk to counsel about which standard governs the population, but the practice points protect the youth and the officer either way.

Stevenson versus Nail. Fifth Circuit, decided September 28, 2026, docket number 26-30081. This decision is unpublished, so it is not binding precedent. Here are the facts. A Louisiana prisoner sued an officer for excessive force. The district court ruled that the written statement from his cellmate was not competent summary judgment evidence and granted summary judgment for the officer. The holding. Affirmed. The court pointed to the summary judgment rule and the federal statute on declarations made under penalty of perjury. The opinion does not describe the force and does not say whether qualified immunity was the basis. What this means in practice. The form a witness statement takes can decide whether a court will even consider it. An unsworn written statement may not count when it matters most. How to train it. Investigators should take inmate and staff statements promptly and in a usable form: sworn, or made under penalty of perjury. Build the format into the incident investigation checklist so it does not depend on who is working that day.

Johnson versus Tarrant County. Fifth Circuit, decided October 1, 2026, docket number 25-10923. This decision is unpublished, so it is not binding precedent. Here are the facts. A man died of a fentanyl overdose in the Tarrant County jail, allegedly from drugs supplied by a commissary contractor's employee. No individual officers were sued. The holding. Affirmed dismissal of the Monell claim against the county. The family did not plausibly allege a pattern amounting to a de facto policy. Qualified immunity was not at issue. What this means in practice. A single death is not a policy case without a pattern. A documented pattern the agency ignored is what converts one death into county liability. How to train it. Contraband control covers vendors and contractors, not just visitors and staff. Audit contractor access, screening, and supervision the same way inmate movement is audited, and document the fixes when a problem is found.

Hyttinen versus Frantti. Sixth Circuit (from the Western District of Michigan), decided September 30, 2026, docket number 25-2047. This decision is unpublished, so it is not binding precedent. Here are the facts. Michigan state troopers went to serve a civil bench warrant on Jennifer Vaughan at her listed address, which was her aunt and uncle's home. The owners were on vacation and told troopers by phone that Vaughan was somewhere else. On body camera the troopers said their main concern was Vaughan's boyfriend. They heard what they thought was a woman's voice; the owners said it was a television left on for the dog. Lights were on, and a neighbor said a woman had called the dog in the night before. Six officers searched the house for about an hour and found no one. The holding. Affirmed summary judgment and qualified immunity. Under Payton versus New York, entry on an arrest warrant is judged objectively, so the officers' interest in the boyfriend did not count. The officers had a reasonable belief that Vaughan lived there and was inside. Whether Payton requires probable cause or only reasonable belief is unsettled in the Sixth Circuit; the cases using probable cause are unpublished, and unpublished cases cannot clearly establish the law. Steagald did not apply because the person named in the warrant lived in the home. Panel: Judges Siler, Davis, and Ritz, opinion by Judge Ritz. No dissent. What was not decided. The court did not settle the standard. It said the question is open, and because it is open the officers get immunity. What this means in practice. This entry survived because of a gap in the law, not because the court blessed it. If the Sixth Circuit adopts probable cause in a published case, the next officers will not have that protection. The body camera statement about the boyfriend did not sink these troopers only because motive is irrelevant under Payton. How to train it. Train officers to articulate specific, recent facts showing the person named in the warrant lives at the address and is inside right now, and to write those facts down before entry. An arrest warrant is not a tool to find somebody else, and nobody should describe it that way, least of all on camera. Field contacts and warrant pickups in probation work carry the same discipline: know, and document, why you believe the person is inside.

Fambrough versus City of East Cleveland. Sixth Circuit (from the Northern District of Ohio), decided October 1, 2026, docket number 25-3300. This is a published decision. Here are the facts. A political activist's campaign sound truck became the target of city enforcement. At the insistence of the mayor's chief of staff, the police chief emailed all officers to find the vehicle and tow it, cite the operator, and contact the chief to claim a surprise. Officers cited Mr. Fambrough and towed the truck from the street, refusing his offers to drive it away. The truck took about $6,000 in damage. The holding. The court revived the First Amendment retaliation claims against the mayor and the chief of staff under the Nieves and Hartman probable cause rules. For the police chief, it affirmed qualified immunity: no precedent clearly established that acting as a conduit for someone else's retaliatory motive was unlawful. The Monell ruling was vacated and remanded. The Fourth Amendment impoundment claim against the city failed because the street tow served a community caretaking function, illegal parking and public convenience, and followed standardized criteria. Panel: Chief Judge Kethledge and Judges Bush and Nalbandian, opinion by Judge Nalbandian. What was not decided. A tow from a private driveway without judicial process might call for more; that question was not decided. The chief's immunity rests on the law not being clearly established, not on any finding that the conduct was proper. What this means in practice. Officers who carry out a tow-it-and-cite-him order from the top can get pulled into retaliation litigation. A tow is defensible when it is documented as community caretaking under standard criteria, but the motive behind the order can still create liability for whoever gave it. How to train it. Train supervisors that an email asking to be contacted to claim a surprise will be read out loud someday. In a facility, the parallel is any directive that singles out one person for enforcement. Document the neutral reason, apply the standard criteria, and if the reason is not neutral, say so before acting on it.

Andrade versus Collier County Sheriff. Eleventh Circuit (from the Middle District of Florida, captioned Andrade versus Rambosk below), decided October 2, 2026, docket number 25-12396. This decision is unpublished, so it is not binding precedent. A verification note before the facts. The appellate opinion is two sentences and does not recount the facts. The facts below come from the complaint as described in a 2023 district court order, read through a secondary copy rather than a court website. They are allegations, not findings. The district court's final opinion could not be obtained, so whether the ruling rested on qualified immunity, on no constitutional violation, or on something else is unconfirmed. An online summary describes this as a death in custody case; it is not one. Here are the facts. Shortly after one in the morning in September 2020 in Immokalee, Florida, a 911 caller reported a man with a shovel banging on her door. The estate alleges that Nicolas Morales, a Spanish-speaking farm worker, was in a mental health crisis and had dropped the shovel. It alleges that about 21 seconds after deputies arrived, one deputy fired four times from about five feet and hit him three times, that a canine was then released and held on for about a minute, and that only afterward did a deputy call out "less lethal." It alleges a delay in medical aid. He died at the hospital. Claims included deadly force, canine force, failure to intervene, medical care, and a Monell claim. The holding. Per curiam, Chief Judge Pryor and Judges Grant and Luck: affirmed on the basis of the thorough and well-reasoned opinion of the district court. The estate was the appellant, so the ruling for the deputies, the sheriff, and the county stands. No dissent. What was not decided. The Eleventh Circuit wrote nothing of its own on the merits. The opinion is unpublished. This case does not give you a new rule. What this means in practice. The legal outcome favors the defendants, but the reasoning is not something to teach from until the district court opinion can be read. How to train it. Use the alleged timeline as a scenario, not as law. A crisis call, a person holding a tool, a language barrier, seconds on scene, then lethal force first, a dog second, and less lethal last. Teach time and distance, language access, assigning a less lethal role before contact, the decision to deploy a canine after a shooting, and the duty to render aid. Ask trainees what they could do with more time and more distance, and what they would need to articulate afterward.

People versus Floresmata. California Court of Appeal, First District, decided September 30, 2026, docket number A173684. This decision is unpublished, so it is not binding precedent. Here are the facts. During a traffic stop in Atherton that lasted about five minutes, an officer pat-searched the driver and found a handgun. The officer relied on baggy clothing, a dispatch records check showing prior charges for robbery, resisting an officer, and a dangerous weapon, recent probation, and an upcoming consent search of the car with the driver standing nearby. The holding. The frisk was upheld. The court did not decide whether criminal history alone can justify a frisk. Unpublished, so it cannot be cited in California courts. What this means in practice. A frisk holds up when every factor is articulated and the records information came through dispatch rather than memory. How to train it. Teach officers to name each factor that supported the frisk, in order, and to get records information from dispatch so it is logged and verifiable.

Now the application, the part to take into a briefing room or a training block this week.

First, Restraint is a use of force with a clock on it. If a facility secures a person to a fixed object, it needs a stated reason, a time limit, scheduled and logged checks, water, and toilet access, with video retained. Mason v. Talley was a win for the officers by one vote under the Eighth Amendment, and the dissent is a road map for the next plaintiff. In juvenile and pretrial settings the Fourteenth Amendment standard applies, so cap the time, check and document, and offer water and a toilet.

Second, Decontaminate everyone who was exposed, and write it down. Decontamination is part of the deployment, not an afterthought. Edwards v. Brown shows how a single line in a grievance response can follow an agency into a published opinion, and how a two-day denial can come back years later.

Third, Keep records longer than you think you need to. Grievance tolling in Edwards means a claim can arrive after the two-year mark. Deployment reports, decontamination logs, grievances, and video need to exist when it does.

Fourth, Articulate the facts before entry. In Hyttinen the officers were saved by unsettled law, and that is not a plan. Specific, recent facts that the named person lives there and is inside, written down before the door opens.

Fifth, Treat a qualified immunity win on unsettled law as a warning. The officers in Mason, the troopers in Hyttinen, and the police chief in Fambrough all got immunity because the law was not clearly established. Each of those opinions makes the law clearer for the next case, and the next set of facts may not be so lucky.

Sixth, Candor and video decide these cases. In Mason the defense first denied the incident until video surfaced, and no footage was produced for the second day. In Stevenson the case turned on the form of a witness statement. Preserve it, document it, take statements in a sworn form, and never deny what the camera saw. None of this week's cases were PREA cases, but the habits that protect staff there, documented checks, preserved video, and honest reports, are the same ones these opinions reward.

Seventh, Monell liability needs a pattern. Johnson v. Tarrant County failed for lack of a pattern. The best defense is a record showing the agency found problems and fixed them, including problems with vendors and contractors.
