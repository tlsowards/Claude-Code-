### Short 2: Edwards v. Grubbs

- Hook (on screen and spoken): The case turns on the absence of a warning.
- Body: Three mechanical points. First, if policy says warn when feasible, training must define feasible narrowly so officers understand the default is to warn and the exception has to be articulated. Second, add a terrain assessment step to the Taser block that is as automatic as the arc display check: embankments, stairwells, elevated platforms, roadways, water, and moving traffic convert a less lethal option into a potentially lethal one.
- Card: Edwards v. Grubbs, Eleventh Circuit, June 30, 2026

### Short 3: Searles v. City of Houston

- Hook (on screen and spoken): Same doctrine as Edwards, opposite result.
- Body: First, the command and fire interval. The officer started a command and fired before completing it. The Fifth Circuit did not condemn that, but no agency should be comfortable with it.
- Card: Searles v. City of Houston, Fifth Circuit, July 9, 2026

### Short 4: Harold v. Hagan

- Hook (on screen and spoken): Deployment is a decision point, not a momentum event.
- Body: Bite duration and target area are separate liability questions from the decision to deploy. Even a justified release becomes a problem if the call-off is slow or the apprehension lands somewhere it should not. Canine programs need documented call-off standards and a supervisory review that examines duration independently, not as an afterthought to the deployment decision.
- Card: Harold v. Hagan, Fourth Circuit, April 28, 2026 (panel opinion; rehearing petition pending as of the episode)
