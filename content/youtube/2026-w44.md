## Shorts (vertical, under 60 seconds each)

### Short 1: United States v. Zamora

- Hook (on screen and spoken): When a person does not yield, the Fourth Amendment clock does not start until he submits or the officer gets physical control.
- Body: Teach Hodari D. and Torres together, because officers tend to remember only half the rule. A show of authority without submission is not a seizure.
- Card: United States v. Zamora, Ninth Circuit, October 29, 2026
- Close: Full brief and training points in the Sowards Briefing Room, link in bio.

### Short 2: Collazo v. Otsego County

- Hook (on screen and spoken): In a true emergency with an outstanding firearm, courts give officers room to detain an uncooperative person at the scene, and low-level control holds matched to resistance are very defensible.
- Body: Teach the step up and the step down: verbal direction, escort hold, separation from an anchor point like a railing or door frame, handcuffs, and then stop. The moment someone reports an injury or medical condition, change what you are doing and say so on camera. The body camera here captured the officers explaining why they were not leaving: shots fired, gun not found.
- Card: Collazo v. Otsego County, Sixth Circuit, October 29, 2026
- Close: Full brief and training points in the Sowards Briefing Room, link in bio.
- Cut from: the "Collazo v. Otsego County: in practice" and "train it" segments of the long video, or re-record from this text in the same voice.

### Short 3: Merrill v. Seagraves

- Hook (on screen and spoken): A second Taser cycle on a person who is face down and controlled by two officers, without a warning and time to comply, is going to a jury when the facts about whether he was subdued are disputed.
- Body: Every additional Taser cycle is a new use of force that needs its own justification. Tensing up while two officers hold the arms is usually not a reason for another cycle. Warn, give time to comply, transition to hands-on control, and in the report articulate each cycle separately.
- Card: Merrill v. Seagraves, Tenth Circuit, October 27, 2026
- Close: Full brief and training points in the Sowards Briefing Room, link in bio.
- Cut from: the "Merrill v. Seagraves: in practice" and "train it" segments of the long video, or re-record from this text in the same voice.

### Short 4: People v. Ball

- Hook (on screen and spoken): A trained officer who stays calm with a gun pointed at him is still a victim.
- Body: In reports, describe exactly what the suspect did and how a reasonable person would perceive it, then describe the officer's own reaction honestly, whatever it was.
- Card: People v. Ball, California Supreme Court, October 26, 2026
- Close: Full brief and training points in the Sowards Briefing Room, link in bio.
- Cut from: the "People v. Ball: in practice" and "train it" segments of the long video, or re-record from this text in the same voice.

### Short 5: People v. Steele, with In re Steele

- Hook (on screen and spoken): Section 835a gives officers the same reasonableness framework in a criminal case as in a civil one, and a leg sweep on a subject who tenses and drops his weight after repeated orders will hold up.
- Body: Count the warnings and get them on camera; two dozen orders to leave made this case. Keep commentary out of the contact. When arresting on a warrant, confirm it and, when practical, explain it or show it.
- Card: People v. Steele, with In re Steele, California Court of Appeal, Fourth District, Division One, October 30, 2026
- Close: Full brief and training points in the Sowards Briefing Room, link in bio.
- Cut from: the "People v. Steele, with In re Steele: in practice" and "train it" segments of the long video, or re-record from this text in the same voice.

### Short 6: People v. Hicks

- Hook (on screen and spoken): The deputies' quick move from face down to the side position defeated the defense theory.
- Body: When you see stimulant intoxication and a racing pulse, minimize time face down, roll the person to the side as soon as you have control, call for medical, and narrate what you are doing. Attaching a leg strap to handcuffs puts a person in a hog-tie style position, and many agencies restrict or prohibit it because of positional asphyxia risk. If it is ever used, keep it brief, keep the person off his chest, and get him upright or into a restraint chair, as these deputies did.
- Card: People v. Hicks, California Court of Appeal, Second District, Division Two, October 29, 2026
- Close: Full brief and training points in the Sowards Briefing Room, link in bio.
- Cut from: the "People v. Hicks: in practice" and "train it" segments of the long video, or re-record from this text in the same voice.

### Short 7: Turner v. Sims

- Hook (on screen and spoken): A vague warning is not automatically a civil rights violation, but it invites a lawsuit.
- Body: Tell the person the actual consequence, a violation report, a hearing, a return to custody, rather than saying bad things will happen. Professional, specific, consequence-based language protects staff and the people they supervise.
- Card: Turner v. Sims, California Court of Appeal, First District, Division Two, October 26, 2026

Correct answer for the follow-up reply: C. A show of authority without submission is not a seizure. But under Torres v. Madrid, physical force applied with intent to restrain is a seizure the instant it lands, so the justification has to exist when hands go on.
