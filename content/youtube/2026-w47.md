#### Short 1.1: Crowley v. Cassidy

- Question (on screen, first two seconds): What does Crowley v. Cassidy mean on your next shift?
- Hook (on screen and spoken): Crowley is a second officer case, and that is how it should be read.
- Body: The deputy who fired did not see a gun, did not see the man's hands clearly, and fired a few seconds after hearing a partner yell "Gun!" and two shots. The majority held that sequence, inside a half minute struggle the man had started, gave a reasonable officer cause to believe a deadly threat was present. The dissent held that a glance at the man's hands would have shown there was none, and that the circuit's cases already said possession of a gun is not enough.
- Card: Crowley v. Cassidy, Sixth Circuit, November 16, 2026

#### Short 1.2: Crowley v. Cassidy

- Question (on screen, first two seconds): How do you train Crowley v. Cassidy?
- Hook (on screen and spoken): Run Crowley as a two officer scenario and debrief it from both opinions.
- Body: Put the trainee in Beach's position: a partner reaches into a vehicle, the driver grabs the arm, the trainee is pulling on the driver's clothing, a shout of "Gun!" comes from the other side of the body, then two shots. #### Short 1.3: Brown v. Adamson

- Question (on screen, first two seconds): What does Brown v. Adamson mean on your next shift?
- Hook (on screen and spoken): For custody medical staff and the supervisors who sit on review committees, Brown is both a shield and a warning.

- Question (on screen, first two seconds): How do you train Brown v. Adamson?
- Hook (on screen and spoken): Teach Zorn's two requirements as a pair, specificity and factual similarity, using Brown as the custody example alongside the force examples.

#### Short S1.1: Gregory v. Dawdy

- Question (on screen, first two seconds): What does Gregory v. Dawdy mean on your next shift?

#### Short S2.1: Robertson v. United States

- Question (on screen, first two seconds): What does Robertson v. United States mean on your next shift?
- Hook (on screen and spoken): Robertson is a model of a force record that holds up.
- Body: Nearly every fact the court relied on was created or preserved by the officers before the first strike: the warrant for murder with an unrecovered firearm, the twenty minutes of recorded calls to surrender, the attempt to locate him with a camera, the clearing of the other rooms and removal of an occupant, the girlfriend's refusal to say whether he was armed, and the announcement of "Gun!" when the bed came up. The force itself was described with a clock: hands under the body, a refusal to release them, compliance strikes to the ribs, handcuffs, and force stopped within 45 seconds. The injuries matched the force described, and the hospital record itself called the wrist injury likely chronic.

In Crowley v. Cassidy, what did the Sixth Circuit majority say was the key question about Deputy Beach's decision to fire?
A. Whether Beach himself saw Crowley holding a gun
B. Whether Crowley had been suspected of a serious crime before the struggle
C. Whether the sergeant's shout that Crowley had a gun, immediately followed by two gunshots, could have made Beach believe Crowley was trying to use a gun

Answer in the brief: https://app.sowardsconsulting.com/briefs/2026-w47

Correct answer for the follow-up reply: C. The majority assumed Beach never saw a gun and decided the case on what he heard inside a close quarters struggle. The dissent would have required him to look at the hands first. The decision is unpublished, and the sergeant who fired first did not appeal.
