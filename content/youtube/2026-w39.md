## Description

This week is the mirror image of the last one. Three cases again, but in two of them federal appellate courts reached the merits, looked at deadly force that killed a man, and held the force did not violate the Fourth Amendment at all. In Handy v. Jones, the Ninth Circuit held that Anchorage officers acted reasonably when they shot a man who came out with a shotgun, refused commands, and advanced on them waving it overhead, and said no clearly established law requires officers to wait until an armed suspect aims at them. In Calloway v. Adcock, the Eleventh Circuit held that an off-duty Alabama police captain reasonably fired on an unarmed, enraged man who had shrugged off a Taser and was landing head strikes while driving him toward his family. The counterweight, Rosales v. Lewis from the Fifth Circuit, is a loss on the stop and frisk, not on force. The Supreme Court was in recess.

## Shorts (vertical, under 60 seconds each)

### Short 1: Handy v. Jones

- Hook (on screen and spoken): The why matters more than the what.
- Body: Have officers articulate the accumulation in order, out loud: what dispatch said, what they saw, what they ordered, what the subject did, and what changed. Do not let anyone reduce this opinion to a slogan about not having to wait; the advance did the work, and the alternative holding is narrower than it sounds. Run the hypothetical from the episode: a shots-fired call, a woman heard screaming, a man in the front yard holding a rifle at low ready who does not drop it and does not raise it.
- Card: Handy v. Jones, Ninth Circuit, September 15, 2026

### Short 2: Calloway v. Adcock

- Hook (on screen and spoken): Unarmed does not mean not deadly.
- Body: When officers write an unarmed subject report, the narrative has to carry disparity of force, weapon ineffectiveness, the officer's escape route or lack of one, and what was behind the officer. Document device failure in the sentence where it happened, not in a supplemental: the Taser was deployed, it connected, and the subject pulled the probes out. Crisis intervention training should say both halves out loud, that a man off his medications is both a patient and, in that moment, a lethal danger; officers taught only the first half hesitate in the wrong moment, and officers taught only the…

In Handy v. Jones, what did the Ninth Circuit say about a rule requiring officers to wait until an armed suspect aims at them?
A. Officers must wait until the muzzle comes up before using deadly force
B. No clearly established law requires officers to hold their fire until an armed suspect actually aims a weapon at them
C. Any armed subject is automatically a lethal threat

Answer in the brief: https://app.sowardsconsulting.com/briefs/2026-w39

Correct answer for the follow-up reply: B. The panel said no wait-to-be-aimed-at rule exists, but the holding is narrower than it sounds. The facts included an advance toward officers after refused commands, and the opinion does not say a static armed subject may be fired on.
