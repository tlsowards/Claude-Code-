## Description

```
Two items this week, and they share one fault line. In Smith v. Olvera, decided July 14, a divided Ninth Circuit panel granted qualified immunity to an Elko, Nevada officer who struck a prone, just-crashed suspect in the head with a baton in the dark. The majority ruled only that no earlier case put the officer on notice; it did not say the head strikes were lawful. Judge Thomas dissented, writing that whether the man was taking furtive or threatening actions or was simply lying there incapacitated was a jury question. On June 22, the Supreme Court declined to hear the City of Los Angeles's appeal in the McBride case, leaving in place a six to five en banc ruling that a jury could find the last two shots, fired after a knife-wielding man had fallen, were excessive. Both cases turn on the moment a threat may have ended and force continued.
```

### Short 1: Smith v. Olvera

- Hook (on screen and spoken): A baton strike to the head is not pain compliance.
- Body: Four points. First, lead with this: head strikes with an impact weapon are deadly force. Teach target zones that keep non-compliance responses off the head, neck, and throat unless a genuine deadly threat justifies lethal force.
- Card: Smith v. Olvera, Ninth Circuit, July 14, 2026

### Short 2: The McBride case (City of Los Angeles petition for certiorari)

- Hook (on screen and spoken): In the litigation that follows a fatal shooting, the fight will very often be about the final rounds, the ones fired after the subject started to fall.
- Body: Train officers to shoot to stop the threat and then to assess, not to empty a magazine on a plan formed a second and a half earlier. Shoot or strike to stop the threat, then read the threat again before the next application. Train command staff that the final rounds are where the case will be fought, and that under the Barnes totality framework the transition point, where a threat may have ended and force continued, is where courts are looking.
- Card: The McBride case (City of Los Angeles petition for certiorari), Supreme Court of the United States, reviewing the en banc Ninth Circuit, June 22, 2026 (certiorari denied)
