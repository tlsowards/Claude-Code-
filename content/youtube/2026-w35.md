## Shorts (vertical, under 60 seconds each)

### Short 1: DeWeese v. Hancock

- Hook (on screen and spoken): Three facts, stacked in order, produced immunity: the subject was armed, he failed to comply with lawful orders, and he was warned of the impending use of force.
- Body: For line officers, the canine warning is not a courtesy. It is a documented decision point that changes the constitutional analysis. Make it audible, specific about what is going to happen, and leave a real window to comply.
- Card: DeWeese v. Hancock, Tenth Circuit, August 19, 2026
- Close: Full brief and training points in the Sowards Briefing Room, link in bio.

### Short 2: Spencer v. Glaser

- Hook (on screen and spoken): This is the case that gives an officer a defensible way to talk about the second and third rounds.
- Body: Run reassessment drills where the threat genuinely ends: subject goes down, stays down, hands visible, no movement. Officers need reps at stopping, not only at shooting. In reports, articulate each shot from observable conduct and carry the earlier conduct into how the next movement was read.
- Card: Spencer v. Glaser, Fourth Circuit, August 4, 2026
- Close: Full brief and training points in the Sowards Briefing Room, link in bio.
- Cut from: the "Spencer v. Glaser: in practice" and "train it" segments of the long video, or re-record from this text in the same voice.

### Short 3: Harold v. Hagan

- Hook (on screen and spoken): A canine is a weapon for constitutional purposes.
- Body: Train handlers on force proportionality, not just on canine tactics. Before the send, ask whether the subject is contained, whether he is resisting at all, and what the dog adds that the surrounding officers cannot do by hand. A medical episode and a visible disability belong in that calculus.
- Card: Harold v. Hagan, Fourth Circuit, Late April 2026 (exact date not stated in the source)
- Close: Full brief and training points in the Sowards Briefing Room, link in bio.
- Cut from: the "Harold v. Hagan: in practice" and "train it" segments of the long video, or re-record from this text in the same voice.

### Short 4: Cameron v. City of Des Moines

- Hook (on screen and spoken): Danger does not excuse the warning.
- Body: Build the warning into the release sequence so that skipping it is a conscious, articulated exception rather than a habit. If the handler skips it, the report has to say why a warning was unsafe or impractical on those facts; the defense here lost that claim because nobody explained it. Articulate duration and removal separately, with the time to handcuffing and the reason for the hold.
- Card: Cameron v. City of Des Moines, Eighth Circuit, February 26, 2026

Correct answer for the follow-up reply: B. The panel skipped the first prong entirely. Armed, failed to comply with lawful orders, and warned of the impending use of force were the three facts that produced immunity, but the opinion says nothing about whether the force was reasonable, so it is not a training standard.
