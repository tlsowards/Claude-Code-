## Shorts (vertical, under 60 seconds each)

### Short 1: Edwards v. Brown

- Hook (on screen and spoken): A pepper spray claim can arrive more than two years after the deployment and still be timely.
- Body: Write the decontamination policy around exposure, not around who was the target. After every deployment, staff should ask who else was in the air, including the open-front cell next door and the shared dayroom, and offer and log decontamination for each person. Teach staff not to argue with someone who is coughing and burning about whether they were the intended target.
- Card: Edwards v. Brown, Ninth Circuit, September 28, 2026

### Short 2: Mason v. Talley

- Hook (on screen and spoken): Read this as a warning, not an approval.
- Body: If a facility ever secures a person to a fixed object, policy needs a stated reason, a time limit, scheduled checks at a set interval, access to water and a toilet, and documentation of every one of those. A check is an assessment that gets logged, not a glance through a window. If a one-hour recreation restraint runs past the hour, a supervisor is notified.
- Card: Mason v. Talley, Fourth Circuit, September 28, 2026
- Close: Full brief and training points in the Sowards Briefing Room, link in bio.

### Short 3: Stevenson v. Nail

- Hook (on screen and spoken): The form a witness statement takes can decide whether a court will even consider it.
- Body: Investigators should take inmate and staff statements promptly and in a usable form: sworn, or made under penalty of perjury. Build the format into the incident investigation checklist so it does not depend on who is working that day.
- Card: Stevenson v. Nail, Fifth Circuit, September 28, 2026

### Short 4: Johnson v. Tarrant County

- Hook (on screen and spoken): A single death is not a policy case without a pattern.
- Body: Contraband control covers vendors and contractors, not just visitors and staff. Audit contractor access, screening, and supervision the same way inmate movement is audited, and document the fixes when a problem is found.
- Card: Johnson v. Tarrant County, Fifth Circuit, October 1, 2026

### Short 5: Hyttinen v. Frantti

- Hook (on screen and spoken): This entry survived because of a gap in the law, not because the court blessed it.
- Body: Train officers to articulate specific, recent facts showing the person named in the warrant lives at the address and is inside right now, and to write those facts down before entry. An arrest warrant is not a tool to find somebody else, and nobody should describe it that way, least of all on camera. Field contacts and warrant pickups in probation work carry the same discipline: know, and document, why you believe the person is inside.
- Card: Hyttinen v. Frantti, Sixth Circuit, September 30, 2026

### Short 6: Fambrough v. City of East Cleveland

- Hook (on screen and spoken): Officers who carry out a tow-it-and-cite-him order from the top can get pulled into retaliation litigation.
- Body: Train supervisors that an email asking to be contacted to claim a surprise will be read out loud someday. In a facility, the parallel is any directive that singles out one person for enforcement. Document the neutral reason, apply the standard criteria, and if the reason is not neutral, say so before acting on it.
- Card: Fambrough v. City of East Cleveland, Sixth Circuit, October 1, 2026

### Short 7: Andrade v. Collier County Sheriff

- Hook (on screen and spoken): The legal outcome favors the defendants, but the reasoning is not something to teach from until the district court opinion can be read.
- Body: Use the alleged timeline as a scenario, not as law. A crisis call, a person holding a tool, a language barrier, seconds on scene, then lethal force first, a dog second, and less lethal last. Teach time and distance, language access, assigning a less lethal role before contact, the decision to deploy a canine after a shooting, and the duty to render aid.

- Body: Teach officers to name each factor that supported the frisk, in order, and to get records information from dispatch so it is logged and verifiable.

In Mason v. Talley, why did the Fourth Circuit analyze the shackling under the Eighth Amendment instead of the Fourteenth?
A. The jail's policy required it
B. Mason was a convicted prisoner, not a pretrial detainee

Answer in the brief: https://app.sowardsconsulting.com/briefs/2026-w40

Correct answer for the follow-up reply: B. The standard follows the person's legal status. Pretrial detainees and juveniles are analyzed under the Fourteenth Amendment and the objective reasonableness test of Kingsley v. Hendrickson, so Mason is not permission for juvenile or pretrial facilities.
