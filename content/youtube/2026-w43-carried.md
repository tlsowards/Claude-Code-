## Shorts (vertical, under 60 seconds each)

### Short 1: Handy v. Jones

- Hook (on screen and spoken): An armed, belligerent person can pose an immediate threat even when the gun is never pointed at an officer.
- Body: Teach the vocabulary of the weapon. Not that he had a gun, but where the muzzle was pointed, what the hands were doing, which way the person was moving, and what he said or did in response to commands. Teach that defiance is not a threat by itself.
- Card: Handy v. Jones, Ninth Circuit, September 15, 2026
- Close: Full brief and training points in the Sowards Briefing Room, link in bio.
- Cut from: the "Handy v. Jones: in practice" and "train it" segments of the long video, or re-record from this text in the same voice.

### Short 2: Reinink v. Hart

- Hook (on screen and spoken): The Supreme Court has not said whether an officer's intent affects which force standard applies, or whether a reasonable mistake about the munition supports immunity.
- Body: Teach officers that the intent question is unresolved at the Supreme Court and that the Sixth Circuit's answer is the one in force there. Do not teach a cert denial as if it were a ruling. Pull each opinion from the court's own website before citing it in a lesson plan.
- Card: Reinink v. Hart, Supreme Court of the United States, May 26, 2026
- Close: Full brief and training points in the Sowards Briefing Room, link in bio.
- Cut from: the "Reinink v. Hart: in practice" and "train it" segments of the long video, or re-record from this text in the same voice.

### Short 3: Ruffin v. Davis

- Hook (on screen and spoken): Armed alone is not enough under Ruffin.
- Body: Teach the pause. Ruffin involved 10 shots at a person who was running, and the question after each shot is the same as it has always been: what is he doing right now? For command staff, review how reports and interviews capture the final second.
- Card: Ruffin v. Davis, Fourth Circuit, April 29, 2026
- Close: Full brief and training points in the Sowards Briefing Room, link in bio.
- Cut from: the "Ruffin v. Davis: in practice" and "train it" segments of the long video, or re-record from this text in the same voice.

### Short 4: Hart v. City of Grand Rapids

- Hook (on screen and spoken): In the Sixth Circuit, the force is judged by what the munition could do at that range, not by what the officer meant to fire.
- Body: Load verification is a use of force skill. An officer issued a launcher that can fire both a crowd control round and a round that can injure badly at close range must be able to identify the round by sight and by procedure every time. Teach the witnessed load as a safety control, not paperwork; the policy that required a second person to witness the load was violated here, and that violation is in the record.
- Card: Hart v. City of Grand Rapids, Sixth Circuit, May 15, 2025

In Handy v. Jones, why did the Ninth Circuit find Handy posed an immediate threat even though he never pointed the shotgun at an officer?
A. Because any person holding a long gun is an immediate threat
B. Because he was waving the shotgun overhead while advancing toward the officers after refusing orders to drop it, following a report of possible shots fired

Correct answer for the follow-up reply: B. Officers do not have to wait for a suspect to aim a gun at them before they act. The combination of a visible weapon, defiance of commands, a violent call history, and forward movement is what made the threat immediate.
