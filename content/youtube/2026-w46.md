## Shorts (vertical, under 60 seconds each)

### Short 1: Dickinson v. Trump

- Hook (on screen and spoken): For agencies in the Ninth Circuit this is the clearest recent statement of when a crowd may be dispersed with less lethal tools: an objectively reasonable conclusion that there is a clear and present danger of riot, disorder, interference with traffic, or another immediate threat to public safety, peace, or order, judged by the demonstration as a whole.
- Body: Teach the dispersal decision and the targeting decision as two decisions with two articulations. The dispersal articulation names the objective conditions: what the crowd as a whole was doing, which laws were being broken, what was blocked, thrown, or damaged, what orders were given and ignored, and why the event had turned from protest to disorder. The targeting articulation for any impact round names the person, the act, the distance, the expected point of impact, and who was beside and behind the target.
- Card: Dickinson v. Trump, Ninth Circuit, November 10, 2026
- Close: Full brief and training points in the Sowards Briefing Room, link in bio.

### Short 2: Reach Community Development v. United States Department of Homeland Security

- Hook (on screen and spoken): Chemical agents travel, and the people who complain are often not the people being dispersed.
- Body: Add a drift and bystander block to the chemical agent decision: wind direction and speed, downwind residences and sensitive occupancies, time of day, and alternatives such as line movement, targeted arrests, or impact munitions aimed at specific actors. Have the incident commander log that assessment before authorizing gas, and open the deployment articulation with the crowd's conduct and the specific threats, because that is what carried the day here.
- Card: Reach Community Development v. United States Department of Homeland Security, Ninth Circuit, November 10, 2026

### Short 3: Jehangir v. East

- Hook (on screen and spoken): Officers guarding an arrestee at a hospital are doing legitimate custody work, and the court treated a running camera during that work as part of the job.
- Body: Add a hospital custody scenario to body camera training. The camera stays on for the custody and the medical encounter; officers redirect the lens when an arrestee is exposed for medical reasons; no one comments on the exposure; the footage is flagged for restricted access. Teach the covering move: a blanket replaced, a curtain drawn, a request to staff.
- Card: Jehangir v. East, Ninth Circuit, November 10, 2026
- Close: Full brief and training points in the Sowards Briefing Room, link in bio.

### Short 4: Roybal v. Griffith

- Hook (on screen and spoken): The level of scrutiny for jail policies that classify by sex or transgender status in housing and searches is unsettled, and the Supreme Court has signaled that the Tenth Circuit's analysis needs another look.
- Body: Teach intake searches as two questions: who searches whom, which command staff and counsel set and revisit as the law changes, and how every search is done, which belongs to every deputy. Require a second staff member for any unclothed search, forbid commentary, and document who was present. Brief classification staff that housing decisions for transgender detainees must follow current policy and the PREA individualized assessment, and treat any allegation of sexual comments or threats during a search as a PREA report and a misconduct investigation the day it is made.
- Card: Roybal v. Griffith, Supreme Court of the United States, November 9, 2026

Under Dickinson v. Trump, when may officers in the Ninth Circuit use less lethal munitions to disperse a crowd?
A. Only when a specific person poses an imminent threat of physical harm
B. When there are objectively reasonable grounds to conclude the demonstration as a whole presents a clear and present danger of riot, disorder, interference with traffic, or another immediate threat to public safety, peace, or order

Answer in the brief: https://app.sowardsconsulting.com/briefs/2026-w46

Correct answer for the follow-up reply: B. The court applied Puente v. City of Phoenix and rejected the district court's imminent threat of physical harm rule. The decision concerns dispersing the crowd under the First Amendment. It does not decide whether any particular round aimed at any particular person was reasonable under the Fourth Amendment, which Johnson v. Adgar addressed two weeks earlier.
