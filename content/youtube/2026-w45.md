### Shorts for this video (vertical, under 60 seconds each)

No-repeat check: no earlier kit has Shorts for Johnson v. Adgar. All of these are new.

#### Short 1.1: Johnson v. Adgar

- Question (on screen, first two seconds): What does Johnson v. Adgar mean on your next shift?
- Hook (on screen and spoken): A launcher round that hits the wrong person is not an accident outside the Fourth Amendment.
- Body: If the officer fired on purpose at a group, everyone in the group is an object of that force. The jury's finding that the fire was indiscriminate decided this case, and the court said it did not matter that Adgar had been ordered to shoot only bottle throwers if the jury could find he did not follow that order. The officer won immunity only because no earlier case involved a crowd that had itself thrown objects at police.
- Card: Johnson v. Adgar, Ninth Circuit, November 2, 2026

#### Short 1.2: Johnson v. Adgar

- Question (on screen, first two seconds): How do you train Johnson v. Adgar?
- Hook (on screen and spoken): Teach the 40mm launcher as a point target tool, never an area tool.
- Body: The articulation for every round is a named person doing a specific act, the distance, the expected point of impact, and who is behind and beside the target. If the operator cannot identify the target, the operator holds. Use a spotter or team leader to designate the target out loud before the launch, which separates the decision from the trigger and creates a record.

In Johnson v. Adgar, why was Johnson seized even though the officer did not aim at him?
A. Because any injury caused by police is a seizure
B. Because the officer intentionally fired into a group Johnson was part of and the round limited his movement
C. Because the officer was ordered to fire

Answer in the brief: https://app.sowardsconsulting.com/briefs/2026-w45

Correct answer for the follow-up reply: B. Under Nelson v. City of Davis, a person in a group that officers deliberately fire into is an object of that force. The jury found the fire was indiscriminate, and the jury's reasonable reading of the evidence controls after a verdict.
